# Business need for RideBound's ETA-change split and cap: sources, for and against

Prepared 2026-10-05/06 for the advisor's question: "Your system seems to serve the system side more than UX. What do these metrics bring a business? Do other researchers or companies want to optimize this?"

## How this was gathered

- Every source listed under "Opened sources" was opened during this session. Usually that means WebFetch. Where WebFetch returned HTTP 403 or the PDF could not be parsed, I downloaded the same URL directly and extracted the text (`curl` + `pdftotext`). Each entry says which method was used under "Access".
- Every quote marked VERBATIM was checked character by character against the downloaded text or PDF, not against WebFetch's model summary. One example of why this mattered: WebFetch summarized Pace Chicago's paratransit window as "-10/+20", but the source actually says "(0/+20)".
- For three journal articles whose publisher pages blocked access, I only read the abstract, through the Crossref metadata record (`api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`). These are labelled "abstract only". I quote no numbers from them beyond what the abstract says.
- Evidence levels used: field/real-data, natural experiment, A/B test, survey/stated preference, simulation/model, product policy/terms, regulation/contract, vendor claim/blog.
- I did not use the sources the brief excluded (Timefold, Google InjectedSolutionConstraint, Lyft scheduled-rides help page, Via 2025-04-02 update, Fielbaum & Alonso-Mora 2020, Elmi et al. 2026, Schulz & Pfeiffer 2026, Alonso-Gonzalez et al. 2020, Koenig & Grippenkoven, Uber DeepETA).
- In each "For RideBound" line, "split" means RideBound's division of an ETA change into a dispatch-caused part and an exogenous part. "Cap" means RideBound's per-rider limit on the dispatch-caused part (for example 30 s).

## Q1. Real-data evidence that ETA error, lateness against a promise, or pooled detours cause business harm

### Q1-1. Cohen, Fiszer & Kim (2022), Via field experiments (the strongest single source)

- **Citation:** Cohen, M. C., Fiszer, M. D., & Kim, B. J. (2022). Frustration-Based Promotions: Field Experiments in Ride-Sharing. *Management Science* 68(4):2432-2464. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.3991. Published online 31 Aug 2021. Co-author Fiszer is affiliated with Via Transportation Inc.
- **Access:** author PDF https://maxccohen.github.io/Frustration-Based-Promotions.pdf, opened via WebFetch and saved, text extracted with pdftotext. The INFORMS page returned 403.
- **Evidence level:**
  - Field/real-data: Via NYC rides, 1 May to 31 Dec 2017.
  - Field experiment, which is an A/B-type randomized test: three experiments, in NYC, Washington DC and NYC.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Data set: "our data set includes all the rides completed in NYC between May 1 and December 31, 2017."
  - Definition of ETA error: "It is clear that if the driver arrives later than the proposed ETA, it affects the quality of service. We call this type of frustration a (positive) ETA error."
  - Via separates the parts of travel time it controls from the parts it does not: "Note that factors such as number of stops or itinerary can be controlled by Via, whereas factors such as weather cannot. Our goal is to capture an aggregated measure that is normalized for uncontrolled factors." The paper continues: "The VGR metric allows us to normalize for uncontrolled factors and to isolate the effects related to the quality of service." VGR is the Via travel time divided by the Google Maps estimate for the same trip in a private car at the same time.
  - Pooled-ride frustrations: "Customers may thus experience several types of frustrations, such as long waiting times, a high number of stops, significant detours, driver no-shows, and poor service interaction with the driver."
  - Baseline accuracy: "the average ETA error amounts to 0.404 minutes (i.e., 24.24 seconds) with a standard deviation of 2.172, which means that riders wait on average less than 25 seconds more than the proposed ETA."
  - Rarity of big errors: "Note that ETA errors greater than 10 minutes occur in less than 0.34% of the rides in our data set."
  - Correlation with later use (historical data): "As expected, we find that both ETA error and VGR in the previous week have a negative impact on the current riders' engagement." The authors add: "We refrain to claim that this result is causal since ETA error and VGR may naturally be endogenous".
  - Matched comparison (propensity-score matching plus difference-in-differences, 961 frustrated vs 961 matched non-frustrated riders): "the frustration has a negative impact on future engagement. This effect is likely to be causal since we accounted for the endogeneity of frustrations in the matching analysis."
  - Table 5 coefficients on 1{Frustrated}x1{After-experiment}:
    - Total-rides: "-0.064*** (0.015)"
    - Total-spending: "-0.202** (0.084)"
    - Equation 4 uses t = day.
  - Value of compensating: "riders in the Credit condition will spend on average an extra 11.98% relative to riders in the Control condition."
  - Compensation level: "$5 credit (which is a typical compensation level for the platform)".
  - Compensation practice: "Via's member service associates respond live to rider texts to help solve issues, provide context, and have considerable discretion to compensate proactively."
  - Abstract: compensation "works well for long waiting times but not for long travel times".
- **Note:** Table 1 (historical correlation) reports an ETA-error coefficient of about -0.001 per minute of weekly-average ETA error and a log(VGR) coefficient of about -0.017 to -0.020 on log rides and log spending. I read these off a PDF table whose text extraction is jumbled, so re-check them against the PDF before citing.
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** a pooled-ride operator logs ETA error against the shown ETA and treats it as a business problem. Riders who suffer it ride and spend less afterwards. The operator pays credits for it. Via itself separates delay it controls (stops, itinerary) from delay it does not (weather) when it judges service quality.
  - **Does not show:** that riders react to *mid-ride ETA changes caused by inserting a co-rider*, or that a 30 s cap on dispatch-caused change matters. Via's frustration threshold was 10 min (8 min in DC), far above RideBound's 30 s scale.

### Q1-2. Goldszmidt et al. (2020), Lyft randomized wait-time and price experiments

- **Citation:** Goldszmidt, A., List, J. A., Metcalfe, R. D., Muir, I., Smith, V. K., & Wang, J. (2020). The Value of Time in the United States: Estimates from Nationwide Natural Field Experiments. NBER Working Paper 28208, December 2020. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28208/w28208.pdf
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), text extracted with pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** natural field experiment (randomized) on Lyft. Experiment 1 ran December 2015 to January 2016 with 720,059 customers. Experiment 2 ran April to June 2017 with 3.3 million passengers.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Design: "customers are randomly assigned to wait at the market wait time or at least an additional 60, 150, or 240 seconds over the market wait time".
  - Value of time: "We find that the VOT is roughly $19 per hour (or 75% (100%) of the after-tax mean (median) wage rate)".
  - Elasticities: "Using the full sample where the overall average request rate is 64.2%, we find estimates implying that wait time and price elasticities of demand are -0.0427 and -0.5942, respectively."
  - Size of the treatment: "our high ETA treatment increases the ETAs by an average of approximately 1.6 minutes, an increase of about 52%."
  - ETAs already move before a request: "ETAs vary over the course of a session in 51.3% of sessions ... in 76.4% of sessions, the difference between the maximum and minimum ETAs shown is one minute or less."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** real, randomized evidence that longer shown pickup ETAs reduce ride requests. It also gives a dollar value of rider waiting time ($19.38/h at baseline).
  - **Does not show:** anything about changes *after* commitment, in-ride delays, or pooled rides.
  - **Counter-evidence:** the very small wait elasticity (-0.0427) suggests that small ETA increases cost little conversion before the request (see Q4).

### Q1-3. Liang et al. (2025), food-delivery cancellations measured against expected wait

- **Citation:** Liang, J., Zhao, Y., Wang, H., Yang, L., & Ke, J. (2025). Understanding order cancellation behavior in on-demand delivery services. *Transportation Research Part A* 198, 104515. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2025.104515
- **Access:** TRID abstract page https://trid.trb.org/View/2560936, opened via WebFetch. Verbatim text verified by direct download. Abstract only.
- **Evidence level:** field/real-data (platform orders, survival analysis).
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Data: "Based on a dataset of delivered and cancelled orders from an on-demand food delivery platform in Asia, the authors reveal several key findings."
  - Main finding: "First, the authors identify an asymmetric effect of accumulated waiting time on cancellations before and after the expected waiting time, which provides evidence to the existence of reference-dependence preferences in on-demand services."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** real-data support that customers judge waiting against the *expected* (communicated) time and react asymmetrically once it is exceeded. This supports measuring burden relative to what was promised.
  - **Does not show:** that the *cause* of the overrun matters to the customer, or any ride-pooling effect size.

### Q1-4. Harter, Stich & Spann (2025), deviation from communicated delivery time and repurchase

- **Citation:** Harter, A., Stich, L., & Spann, M. (2025). The Effect of Delivery Time on Repurchase Behavior in Quick Commerce. *Journal of Service Research* 28(2):211-227. https://doi.org/10.1177/10946705241236961. Online 4 Mar 2024.
- **Access:** Crossref record via WebFetch, also downloaded directly. Abstract only.
- **Evidence level:** field/real-data (transaction data), plus an online experiment that only provides the mechanism.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Data: "The authors study the effects of delivery time deviations on repurchase behavior using a large, customer-level, transaction data set from a Western European food delivery service and a controlled online experiment."
  - Findings: "The results show that late (early) deliveries increase (decrease) interpurchase times; these effects diminish with larger deviations. The results also show that late deliveries have a stronger effect on repurchase behavior than early deliveries of the same magnitude."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** being late against the *communicated* time causes measurable repurchase harm, and lateness weighs more than earliness.
  - **Does not show:** effect sizes (they are not in the abstract), or anything about pooled rides.

### Q1-5. Mao et al. (2025), meal-delivery dispatch and repeat orders

- **Citation:** Mao, W., Ming, L., Rong, Y., Tang, C. S., & Zheng, H. (2025). Faster Deliveries and Smarter Order Assignments for an On-Demand Meal Delivery Platform. *Journal of Operations Management* 71(2):220-245. https://doi.org/10.1002/joom.1354. Published 20 Feb 2025.
- **Access:** Crossref record via WebFetch, plus direct download. Abstract only. Wiley and SSRN pages failed.
- **Evidence level:**
  - Field/real-data, with instrumental variables.
  - Simulation, for the dispatch part.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - "Using transactional data from an online meal delivery platform in China, we empirically investigate the asymmetric effects of early and late deliveries on customer repurchasing behavior."
  - "The survival analysis shows that late deliveries significantly reduce future orders, while early deliveries provide only limited benefits."
  - "Guided by these empirical insights, we develop a simulation-based evaluation of different order dispatch algorithms, revealing that maximizing future orders, rather than minimizing delivery time or delays, yields the highest future orders."
- **For RideBound:** the closest published analogue to the advisor's question.
  - **Shows:** researchers explicitly argue that dispatch algorithms should optimize the customer-retention effect of lateness, not only delay totals. This is a business case for "UX-aware dispatch".
  - **Does not show:** the dispatch-caused vs exogenous split, or any ride-pooling result. Its dispatch results are simulated.

### Q1-6. Deshpande & Pendem (2023), Tmall/Cainiao delivery against promise, ratings and sales

- **Citation:** Deshpande, V., & Pendem, P. K. (2023). Logistics Performance, Ratings, and Its Impact on Customer Purchasing Behavior and Sales in E-Commerce Platforms. *Manufacturing & Service Operations Management* 25(3):827-845. https://doi.org/10.1287/msom.2021.1045
- **Access:** Crossref record via WebFetch. Abstract only.
- **Evidence level:** field/real-data, more than 15 million orders.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Data: "Using an extensive data set of more than 15 million customer orders on the Tmall platform and Cainiao network (logistics arm of Alibaba)".
  - Lateness against promise: "Similarly, we find that priority customers (those with two-day and one-day promise speed) provide lower logistics ratings for delivery times longer than their anticipated delivery date."
  - Sales effect: "We estimate that reducing the delivery time of all three-day delivered orders on this platform (which makeup [Formula: see text] 35% of the total orders) to two days would improve the average daily third-party seller sales by 13.3% on this platform." The "[Formula: see text]" placeholder is in the Crossref text.
- **For RideBound:** adjacent evidence that missing a promised date lowers ratings, and ratings drive sales. Time scales (days) and setting (parcels) are far from ride-pooling.

### Verdict for Q1

- **The problem exists and businesses pay for it.** Real platform data from a pooled-ride operator (Via) show that waiting longer than the shown ETA lowers later rides and spending. Detour-type slowness (VGR) also correlates negatively. Via pays about $5 credits for these frustrations.
- **The same holds in adjacent sectors.** Food and parcel delivery data (Asia, Western Europe, China, Alibaba) consistently show that being late against the *communicated* time raises cancellations, lengthens time to the next purchase, and lowers ratings and sales. Lateness hurts more than earliness helps. Mao et al. (2025) go further and argue that dispatch should optimize retention rather than raw delay.
- **What the evidence does not show.** I found no real-data study isolating the effect of *dispatch-caused ETA changes during a pooled trip* (insertions and reroutes) on retention. None tests whether customers react differently to dispatch-caused vs traffic-caused changes.
- **Scale gap.** The measured harms start at thresholds of minutes (Via used 10 min), whereas RideBound caps tens of seconds. Lyft's randomized experiment shows that request decisions are only weakly sensitive to wait (elasticity -0.0427).
- **Fair claim for the thesis.** "Broken time promises cost money and retention, and operators track delay they cause separately from uncontrolled factors (Via's VGR)." Not "riders punish dispatch-caused 30 s changes."

## Q2. Do real contracts and regulations separate operator-caused delay from delay outside the operator's control?

### Q2-1. 49 CFR 37.131, the US ADA complementary paratransit service criteria

- **Citation:** 49 CFR 37.131, Service criteria for complementary paratransit, 10-1-24 edition. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title49-vol1-sec37-131.pdf. Also opened: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/37.131
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), extracted with pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** regulation.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - (b)(2), the booking window: "The entity may negotiate pickup times with the individual, but the entity shall not require an ADA paratransit eligible individual to schedule a trip to begin more than one hour before or after the individual's desired departure time."
  - (f)(3)(i), prohibited operational patterns or practices:
    - "(A) Substantial numbers of significantly untimely pickups for initial or return trips;"
    - "(B) Substantial numbers of trip denials or missed trips;"
    - "(C) Substantial numbers of trips with excessive trip lengths."
  - (f)(3)(ii), the exemption: "Operational problems attributable to causes beyond the control of the entity (including, but not limited to, weather or traffic conditions affecting all vehicular traffic that were not anticipated at the time a trip was scheduled) shall not be a basis for determining that such a pattern or practice exists."
- **Correction:** the brief cited this exemption as (f)(3)(iii). In the current text it is (f)(3)(ii).
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** federal regulation explicitly excludes unanticipated weather and traffic from the lateness and excessive-trip-length tests. The cut-off is "not anticipated at the time a trip was scheduled", i.e., relative to what was known when the commitment was made.
  - **Does not show:** a per-rider cap, or anything at the seconds scale. It is a "pattern or practice" test, not a per-trip penalty.

### Q2-2. 49 CFR Part 37, Appendix D (US DOT's official interpretation of 37.131)

- **Citation:** 49 CFR Part 37, Appendix D, Construction and Interpretation of Provisions of 49 CFR Part 37, 10-1-24 edition. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title49-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title49-vol1-part37-appD.pdf
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** regulation (official interpretation).
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Unforeseen events are excused: "Operational problems outside the control of the entity do not count as part of a pattern or practice under this provision. For example, if the vehicle has an accident on the way to pick up a passenger, the late arrival would not count as part of a pattern or practice. If something that could not have been anticipated at the time the trip was scheduled (e.g., a snowstorm, an accident or hazardous materials incident that traps the paratransit vehicle, like all traffic on a certain highway, for hours), the resulting missed trip would not count as part of a pattern or practice."
  - Foreseeable problems are not excused: "On the other hand, if the entity regularly does not maintain its vehicles well, such that frequent mechanical breakdowns result in missed trips or late arrivals, a pattern or practice may exist. This is also true in a situation in which scheduling practices fail to take into account regularly occurring traffic conditions (e.g., rush hour traffic jams), resulting in frequent late arrivals."
  - Shared rides and trip length: "Since paratransit is a shared ride service, paratransit rides between Point A and Point B will usually take longer, and involve more intermediate stops, than a taxi ride between the same two points. However, when the number of intermediate stops and the total trip time for a given passenger grows so large as to make use of the system prohibitively inconvenient, then this provision would be triggered."
- **For RideBound:** the most direct regulatory support for an attribution split.
  - **Shows:** lateness from the operator's *scheduling decisions* is the operator's responsibility, including failing to plan for normal rush-hour traffic. Lateness from unanticipated external events is excused.
  - **Implication for the thesis:** the excused part is "deviation from what could be anticipated when scheduling". It is not "all traffic". RideBound's exogenous component should be defined relative to the travel-time forecast in force when the commitment was made.

### Q2-3. NYC Comptroller audit of Access-A-Ride (MTA paratransit)

- **Citation:** Office of the New York City Comptroller (2016). Audit Report of the Metropolitan Transportation Authority's Oversight of the Access-A-Ride Program, FK15-098A, May 17, 2016. https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/FK15_098A.pdf
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:**
  - Regulation/contract: the audit quotes contract articles.
  - Field/real-data: the auditors analysed 9.4 million GPS-recorded times.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Contract structure: "Dedicated Service contracts provide for a system of liquidated damages, or credits, for performance deficiencies, such as failing to pick-up customers on time, and incentives for exceeding specified minimum standards."
  - Standard: "DSCs are required to pick-up customers within 30 minutes of scheduled times in a minimum of 92 percent of scheduled trips each month."
  - Penalty article: Article 106(A)(1) provides that in "any month in which the Contractor fails to achieve 92% On Time Performance, the Contractor shall pay the Authority the sum of $10.00 for each trip that occurred fifteen (15) minutes or more past the Scheduled Pickup Window."
  - Incentive article: Article 109(A) provides that in "any month in which the Contractor exceeds 95% On Time Performance, the Contractor will be paid an incentive payment of ten (10) cents per completed trip for all completed trips for the month."
  - Beyond-control exclusion (auditor's description of the contract): "PTM Management Corporation (PTM) had only 91.5 percent on time performance and therefore, would have been subject to liquidated damages for late pick-ups that were not due to circumstances beyond its control (e.g., heavy traffic, inclement weather, etc...)."
  - No-show waiver: "Article 104(B)(1)(a) and (b) allow for charges to be waived if no-shows are due to circumstances beyond the DSC's control."
  - Gaming of timestamps: "found that the 14 DSCs changed 2,588,980 of 9,365,948--27.6 percent--AVLM reported times to earlier times."
  - Who schedules: "Paratransit uses ADEPT, its trip reservation, scheduling, and dispatch system". The carriers (DSCs) provide "drivers, dispatchers, and all other personnel". In 2015 "the 14 DSCs received payments totaling $294.3 million."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** a large real contract with money per late trip ($10 per trip at least 15 min past the window when monthly OTP is below 92%) that excuses late pickups caused by circumstances beyond the contractor's control. In this system, scheduling is done by the agency and driving by contractors, so assigning blame for each late pickup decides who pays.
  - **Also shows a risk:** contractors altered timestamps to dodge penalties. Any attribution used for money must rest on tamper-evident data.
  - **Does not show:** a per-rider "dispatch-caused ETA change" metric. Lateness is measured against the promised window, i.e., the deadline-type rule.

### Q2-4. DREDF/TranSystems (2010), FTA-funded Topic Guide on paratransit on-time performance

- **Citation:** Disability Rights Education & Defense Fund and TranSystems Corporation (June 2010). Topic Guide on On-Time Performance in ADA Paratransit. Funded by the Federal Transit Administration. https://dredf.org/ADAtg/OTP.shtml
- **Access:** WebFetch, then a direct download to verify verbatim text.
- **Evidence level:** regulation guidance / practice guide.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Window length: "A pickup window of 30 minutes or less is standard in the industry. FTA has accepted pickup windows up to that length, and found 60 minutes to be too long."
  - Example: "Pace ADA Paratransit Service in Chicago has established a 20-minute on-time window, from the scheduled pickup time to 20 minutes after the scheduled time (0/+20)."
  - Uncontrollable delay: "Because there will inevitably be operational issues beyond transit agency control (due, for example, to unanticipated congestion or road construction, or poor weather), the reality will likely be something less than 100 percent on-time."
  - How contracts handle it: "ADA paratransit service contracts usually include a minimum on-time performance standard (for example, 92 percent) or an acceptable range (for example, 92 to 95 percent). Below 92 percent there may be penalties, and above 95 percent there may be incentives. ... Or due to weather and traffic issues in other months, the contractor may operate at 92 percent, the lower end of the acceptable range, so neither penalties nor incentives are applied."
  - Causes the operator must fix: "The reasons for the poor on-time performance can vary greatly—it could be a lack of drivers that results in run closures and unscheduled trips ... Or it could be a need for better scheduling and/or dispatching."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** the industry accepts that uncontrollable delay exists and that penalties target controllable causes, including scheduling and dispatching.
  - **Counter-point:** the common contract mechanism absorbs exogenous delay *statistically*, through a tolerance band below 100%, rather than by attributing each trip's delay.

### Q2-5. TCRP Synthesis 31 (1998), survey of paratransit contracting practice

- **Citation:** Transit Cooperative Research Program (1998). TCRP Synthesis 31: Paratransit Contracting and Service Delivery Methods. Transportation Research Board. https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/tcrp/tsyn31.pdf
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** survey of agencies (practice survey, not stated preference).
- **VERBATIM:**
  - "To ensure contractor adherence to performance standards, most (67 percent) respondent contracts include penalty clauses stipulating that failure to comply will result in the assessment of liquidated damages."
  - "Generally, penalties are assessed for late, missed, and excessively lengthy trips."
  - On who schedules: "The data revealed that a significant number of transit agencies are performing their own reservation and scheduling functions to control passenger usage; others reported intentions to bring reservation and scheduling in-house."
- **For RideBound:** liquidated damages for late and *excessively long* (detour-heavy) trips are standard. The scheduling party and the operating party are often different organizations, which is exactly when cause attribution matters. The source is old (1998).

### Q2-6. City of El Monte (CA), Transit and Paratransit Operations RFP

- **Citation:** City of El Monte (February 19, 2020). Request for Proposals, Transit and Paratransit Operations. Document marked DRAFT. https://www.ci.el-monte.ca.us/DocumentCenter/View/3726/RFP---2020-Transit-and-Paratransit-Operations
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** contract/RFP.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Window: "The City has set an on-time performance level of 97% for paratransit. On-time performance is defined as arriving to pick up a passenger no more than five (5) minutes before to 15 minutes after the scheduled pick-up time."
  - On-time penalty: "Penalty/Liquidated Damages: One hundred dollars ($100) per month for each full or partial percentage point below the 97% performance target."
  - Productivity target: "The City requires that the Contractor exceed a target of two and a quarter (2.25) passengers per VSH for the first year and two and a half (2.5) passengers per VSH thereafter (monthly average)."
  - Productivity penalty: "Penalty/Liquidated Damages: One thousand dollars ($1,000) per month for having an average of less than 2.25 passengers per VSH for the first year and two and a half (2.5) passengers per VSH thereafter."
  - Excusable reasons (termination clause only): "If it is later determined by the City of El Monte that the Contractor had an excusable reason for not performing, such as a strike, fire, or flood, events which are not the fault of or are beyond the control of the Contractor".
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** an agency pays to enforce *both* sides of the pooling trade-off: punctuality and pooling productivity (passengers per vehicle-hour).
  - **Against a strong claim:** the on-time liquidated damages here contain no per-trip traffic exclusion. The "beyond the control" language appears only in the default/termination clause.

### Q2-7. ATL / Gwinnett County, Transdev invoice adjustments (assessed liquidated damages)

- **Citation:** ATL (Atlanta-region Transit Link Authority), procurement attachment "Attachment 22 - Performance Metrics", containing Transdev Invoice Adjustments for Ride Gwinnett, invoice date January 2025 (posted October 2025). http://atltransit.ga.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Attachment-22-Performance-Metrics.pdf
- **Access:** WebFetch returned 403. Opened by direct download, then pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** contract, applied to real monthly invoices.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - On-time deduction: "$1.00 per revenue hour shall be deducted from the Contractor's monthly service hour billing each month the County's Fixed Route Local service does not attain at least 80% on time performance in a given month."
  - Paratransit missed trips: "The Contractor shall be assessed $1000 if more than .25% (a quarter of a percent) of all requested trips are missed."
  - Traffic exclusion in a different metric: "A failure in service shall be defined as any vehicle incident (excluding traffic and weather) ... which results in a delay of five minutes or more".
- **For RideBound:** real invoices show money deducted for on-time and missed-trip performance. The explicit "excluding traffic and weather" carve-out appears in the vehicle-failure metric, not in the on-time metric. So contracts vary in where they apply attribution.

### Q2-8. DoorDash, "Lateness Based Violations Explained" (courier accountability policy)

- **Citation:** DoorDash Help Center (Dasher Support). Lateness Based Violations Explained. Undated; accessed 2026-10-05. https://help.doordash.com/dashers/s/article/Lateness-Based-Deactivations-Explained
- **Access:** WebFetch returned 403. Opened by direct download of the HTML.
- **Evidence level:** product policy/terms.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Definition of late: "A delivery is considered late when the amount of time taken to travel to the merchant and/or to the customer (e.g., 20 minutes) exceeds the estimated time, taking into account factors outside of your control (e.g., wait time at merchant). It is NOT based on the suggested arrival times in the app (e.g., 1:00PM)."
  - Traffic in the baseline: "We use Google maps to calculate how long each delivery should take, taking into account reasonable and safe travel times and real-time traffic conditions."
  - Platform decisions excluded: "Late orders due to batching (i.e., multiple stacked orders), DoorDash system outages, or other extenuating circumstances are not considered contract violations and are also excluded from this process."
- **For RideBound:** the closest operational analogue to RideBound's split that I found. A dispatch platform:
  - attributes lateness caused by **its own batching decisions** (the analogue of inserting another rider) to itself;
  - adjusts for real-time traffic and merchant wait;
  - holds the courier responsible only for the remainder.
  - **Limit:** the policy decides courier accountability, not what the customer is promised or paid.

### Q2-9. Uber, "Introducing Delayed Ride Guarantee" (driver pay)

- **Citation:** Uber (August 3, 2026). Introducing Delayed Ride Guarantee. Uber blog, US, for drivers. https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/delay-ride-guarantee/
- **Access:** WebFetch for the summary. Verbatim text verified by direct download.
- **Evidence level:** product policy/terms.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Purpose: "When you have a rider in the car, delays happen — traffic, detours, route changes, or unpredictable conditions."
  - Trigger: "After you pickup, if the ride runs 5 minutes or longer than the original estimate, your fare will increase."
  - Rider-added stops: "If a rider adds stops after pickup, those minutes do not count toward the guarantee."
  - Driver's own detours: "The Delayed Ride Guarantee doesn't apply if you take an unnecessary, long detour, such as when you don't follow the suggested navigation instructions and the alternative route you choose is responsible for adding significant delay or distance to the trip."
  - Exclusions: "*This policy will not apply in New York City, Washington state, and Oregon state. It is also excluded from the following products: Hourly, Intercity, Connect, Rentals, High Capacity Vehicles, Taxi (in Boston and Washington D.C. only), and Matched Share Rides."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** Uber attributes in-ride delay by cause for money. Traffic and route changes are compensated, rider-added stops are handled separately, and the driver's own detours are excluded.
  - **Notable exclusion:** matched shared rides are excluded, where insertion-caused delay would complicate attribution. This is a dated (2026) industry instance of exactly the attribution problem RideBound formalizes. It concerns driver pay, not rider promises.

### Q2-10. EU Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 (air passenger rights)

- **Citation:** Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 11 February 2004. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32004R0261
- **Access:** WebFetch. Verbatim text verified by direct download.
- **Evidence level:** regulation.
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Article 5(3): "An operating air carrier shall not be obliged to pay compensation in accordance with Article 7, if it can prove that the cancellation is caused by extraordinary circumstances which could not have been avoided even if all reasonable measures had been taken."
  - Recital 14 examples: "Such circumstances may, in particular, occur in cases of political instability, meteorological conditions incompatible with the operation of the flight concerned, security risks, unexpected flight safety shortcomings and strikes that affect the operation of an operating air carrier."
- **For RideBound:** the clearest example of a law that makes passenger compensation depend on *cause*, with the burden of proof on the operator. As written, Art. 5(3) covers cancellations. Its extension to long delays comes from EU case law, which I did not open, so do not cite that extension from this file.

### Q2-11. ORR (UK rail regulator), Schedule 8 performance regime factsheet

- **Citation:** Office of Rail and Road (17 June 2021). Schedule 8 performance regime: ORR factsheet. https://www.orr.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2021-06/schedule-8-orr-factsheet-june-2021.pdf
- **Access:** WebFetch (PDF saved), pdftotext.
- **Evidence level:** regulation/contract (track access contracts).
- **VERBATIM:**
  - Purpose: "1. To reduce train operators' exposure to losses that arise from delay and cancellations that they cannot control, by compensating them for losses incurred as a result of delay."
  - Payment rule: "Schedule 8 payments are based on attributed delay, benchmarks and payment rates: if a party causes more delay than its benchmarked amount, it pays an amount equal to the excess delay multiplied by a payment rate."
  - Attribution and disputes: "Network Rail is responsible for identifying whether responsibility for incidents causing delays is attributed to Network Rail, to the operator or in some limited circumstances to both parties jointly. Operators are able to challenge this attribution and present alternative evidence, and can ultimately dispute attribution if they wish."
  - Converting minutes to money: "Payment rates in the passenger regime are based on the relationship between disruption and changes in long-term farebox revenue (obtained from econometric analysis) and the average number of passengers estimated to be travelling on affected train services."
- **For RideBound:**
  - **Shows:** a mature, legally required industry system that (1) attributes every delay minute to the responsible party and (2) converts minutes into money through an econometric revenue effect.
  - **Limits:** it is rail between organisations, not ride-pooling between riders. Attribution generates disputes, which is a cost.

### Verdict for Q2

- **The problem exists and money is attached.** US paratransit regulation (49 CFR 37.131(f)(3)(ii) and Appendix D) explicitly separates lateness caused by the operator, including poor *scheduling* that ignores normal rush-hour traffic, from unanticipated external events. Only the former counts.
- **Real contracts follow the same pattern.**
  - Liquidated damages per late trip are standard: 67% of surveyed contracts in 1998; NYC Access-A-Ride contractors pay $10 per late trip; El Monte charges $100 per percentage point.
  - NYC's contract excludes late pickups beyond the contractor's control.
  - Outside transit, a dispatch platform (DoorDash) excludes lateness caused by its own batching decisions from courier accountability.
  - Uber now pays drivers for in-ride delay but excludes driver-caused detours and matched shared rides.
  - UK rail and EU air law price delay by attributed cause.
- **This is direct evidence that operators and regulators need to know *who or what caused* a delay.** RideBound computes exactly that kind of split.
- **What the evidence does not show.**
  - (a) No contract found measures or caps a per-rider, cumulative "dispatch-caused ETA change". Contracts measure lateness against a promised window (the deadline-type rule) and then excuse uncontrollable causes.
  - (b) The usual paratransit mechanism absorbs exogenous delay through a tolerance band (92-95%), not per-trip attribution.
  - (c) Attribution invites disputes and gaming (ORR dispute process; NYC timestamp edits).
- **Fair positioning.** RideBound's split is a candidate *evidence layer* that window-based contracts already need, to decide which late trips are excusable and who pays. The appropriate exogenous baseline is "what could be anticipated at commitment time". RideBound's specific 30 s cap is not something buyers are documented as asking for.
- **Gap:** I did not find an open microtransit (Via/RideCo) contract with on-time definitions and traffic exclusions. Via's Topeka proposal (Q3-4) had no readable SLA text.

## Q4. Counter-evidence

### Q4-1. Grab, chat experiment (riders value timely information)

- **Citation:** Parbat, I., Wang, K., Khan, J., & Tee, M. (March 1, 2018). How Grab Experimented with Chat to Drive Down Booking Cancellations. Grab Tech blog. https://engineering.grab.com/experiment-chat-booking-cancellations
- **Access:** WebFetch, plus direct download.
- **Evidence level:** A/B test (company-reported).
- **VERBATIM:**
  - "We dug into our data to validate the feedback that Service Design team had received and discovered an interesting correlation; bookings which had a GrabChat conversation indeed had a lower likelihood of being cancelled."
  - "We quickly observed that sending out a GrabChat automated-message sooner rather than later was more successful in preventing a booking cancellation."
  - "After thoroughly testing in different cities and verticals, we observed that this small change to the user experience resulted in a reduction of booking cancellations by up to 2 percentage points."
  - "A simple message from the driver-partner could help reduce perceived waiting time for Passengers and give them confidence in the service."
- **For RideBound:** company-reported A/B evidence that *timely communication* reduces cancellations. Some of the business value may come from informing riders promptly rather than from keeping ETAs stable. It does not test stable-but-wrong vs updated-and-accurate ETAs directly.

### Q4-2. Lyft experiments again (Q1-2): low sensitivity to small wait changes at request time

- **VERBATIM:** "wait time and price elasticities of demand are -0.0427 and -0.5942, respectively."
- **Illustration (my arithmetic, not the paper's):** the paper's log-ETA specification and its baseline 3.08-minute ETA imply that +30 s (+16%) changes the request rate by about -0.0427 x ln(3.58/3.08), roughly -0.6% relative.
- **For RideBound:** at the conversion stage, riders barely react to ETA changes of the size RideBound caps. The harms in Q1 appear at minute-scale breaches of a promise, not at tens of seconds.

### Q4-3. Via experiments again (Q1-1): detour frustrations behave differently from waiting frustrations

- **VERBATIM (abstract):** compensation "works well for long waiting times but not for long travel times".
- **For RideBound:** in-vehicle extra time (the main product of insertions) was harder to repair with compensation than pickup waiting. Its commercial lever is weaker or at least different. This cuts both ways: it is an argument to *prevent* in-ride burden, since it cannot be cheaply repaired, but it is not evidence that riders measure it.

### Q4-4. Businesses also pay for utilization, and pooling economics can dominate

- **El Monte (Q2-6):** the penalty for productivity below 2.25 passengers per VSH is "One thousand dollars ($1,000) per month", vs "One hundred dollars ($100) per month for each full or partial percentage point below the 97% performance target" for on-time performance.
- **TechCrunch (Q3-3):** "carpooling has historically been a money pit".
- **Uber's current page (Q3-1)** gives only an average added-time design target, no guarantee.
- **For RideBound:** buyers explicitly pay for pooling efficiency. Firms have exited pooling for economic and demand reasons that better ETA stability may not fix.

### Q4-5. Contracts usually use simpler rules

- **DREDF (Q2-4):** tolerance bands absorb weather and traffic.
- **El Monte, ATL (Q2-6, Q2-7):** on-time liquidated damages without per-trip traffic exclusions.
- **DoorDash, UberEATS (Q3-6):** credits keyed to lateness against the promised time.
- **NYC audit (Q2-3):** attribution data were manipulated (27.6% of AVLM times changed to earlier times).
- **ORR (Q2-11):** attribution requires a dispute process.
- **For RideBound:** the simpler rules ("deadline from promised time", "% on time within window") are what businesses actually contract on. Per-trip attribution has administrative and integrity costs that the thesis should acknowledge.

### Verdict for Q4

- **The problem is real, but fair counter-evidence exists.**
  - (1) Riders value prompt information (Grab −2 pp cancellations), so updating ETAs is not inherently bad.
  - (2) Request decisions are only weakly elastic to small ETA changes (Lyft), so 30 s-scale changes have small direct revenue effects.
  - (3) Detour-type frustrations respond poorly to compensation (Via).
  - (4) Buyers also pay for utilization, and pooling has failed commercially for economic reasons (Lyft exit, "money pit").
  - (5) Contracts mostly rely on deadline/window rules with tolerance bands, not per-trip causal attribution, and attribution invites disputes and gaming.
- **None of this says ETA reliability is unimportant.** It says the business case for RideBound's *specific* split and cap must be argued as an internal control, fairness and attribution tool, not as a direct driver of conversion.

## Best concrete data sources the thesis could actually use

To translate RideBound's simulated ETA changes or lateness into cancellations, retention or money, with their limits.

### 1. Via engagement coefficients (Cohen et al. 2022, Q1-1)

- **What it offers:**
  - Average pickup ETA error of 24.24 s, which is on the same scale as RideBound's 30 s cap. This is a useful sanity anchor: RideBound's cap is about Via's *average* error.
  - Over 10 min of ETA error in under 0.34% of rides.
  - A frustrated-vs-matched difference-in-differences of -0.064 on daily rides and -0.202 on daily spending (Table 5).
  - A historical weekly-ETA-error coefficient of about -0.001 per minute on log rides (Table 1; re-check against the PDF).
  - A typical credit of $5.
- **Limits:** NYC 2017; frustration defined at 8 to 10 min; correlations in Table 1 are not causal; no insertion-caused component is isolated.

### 2. Lyft value of time and wait elasticity (Goldszmidt et al. 2020, Q1-2)

- **What it offers:**
  - $19.38/h at baseline (s.e. $1.39). That gives a money value for rider-seconds: 30 s is about $0.16 per rider at that rate (my arithmetic).
  - Request elasticity to shown wait of -0.0427 at a 64.2% request rate.
- **Limits:** applies to the *displayed pre-request* pickup ETA in 2015 to 2017, not to post-commitment changes or in-vehicle time.

### 3. Contract penalty schedules (Q2-3, Q2-6, Q2-7)

These turn simulated counts of late trips into contract money. They price lateness against a window, so RideBound would first have to convert its outputs into "trips beyond window".

- **NYC Access-A-Ride:**
  - $10 per trip at least 15 min past the window when monthly OTP is below 92%.
  - $0.10 per completed trip incentive when OTP is above 95%.
  - $20 per contractor no-show above 0.3%.
- **El Monte:**
  - $100 per percentage point below 97% OTP.
  - $1,000 per percentage point below 99% trip completion.
  - $1,000 per month below 2.25 passengers per VSH.
- **ATL/Gwinnett:** $1.00 per revenue hour when monthly fixed-route OTP is below 80%; $1,000 when paratransit missed trips exceed 0.25%.

### 4. Consumer credit levels for broken time promises (Q3-6, Q1-1)

- DoorDash: $10 at 10 min or more past the latest time.
- UberEATS (2017): $4.99 at more than 35 min.
- Via: $5 typical credit.
- **Limits:** these are list prices of goodwill, not measured losses. They are discretionary and sometimes limited-time tests.

### 5. Method template: UK rail Schedule 8 (Q2-11)

- **What it offers:** attribute each delay minute to a responsible party, then multiply excess attributed minutes by a payment rate estimated from the econometric effect of lateness on farebox revenue. This is a ready-made, regulator-approved recipe for "dispatch-caused minutes x $/minute".
- **Limit:** rail rates do not transfer. The thesis would need its own rate, for example from sources 1 and 2.

### 6. Public trip datasets

- **NYC TLC High Volume FHV trip records**
  - Data dictionary dated March 18, 2025: https://www.nyc.gov/assets/tlc/downloads/pdf/data_dictionary_trip_records_hvfhs.pdf (opened via WebFetch, pdftotext).
  - Fields include `request_datetime`, `on_scene_datetime` ("Accessible Vehicles-only"), `pickup_datetime`, `dropoff_datetime`, `trip_time`, `shared_request_flag` ("Did the passenger agree to a shared/pooled ride, regardless of whether they were matched? (Y/N)"), `shared_match_flag` ("Did the passenger share the vehicle with another passenger who booked separately at any point during the trip? (Y/N)"), `access_a_ride_flag`, and license codes including "HV0004: Via".
  - **Limits:** no shown ETAs, no cancellations, driver-arrival time only for wheelchair-accessible trips. It can calibrate request-to-pickup times and pooling shares, not promise-breaking.
- **Meituan Hangzhou meal-delivery operational data set**
  - Mao, Ming, Rong, Tang & Zheng (2022). On-Demand Meal Delivery Platforms: Operational Level Data and Research Opportunities. *MSOM* 24(5):2535-2542. https://doi.org/10.1287/msom.2022.1112. Crossref abstract only.
  - VERBATIM: "This data set contains operational level data sampled from July 1 to August 31, 2015, in Hangzhou, China." and "The data set includes information about order placements, order deliveries, restaurants, drivers, weather and traffic conditions, and so on."
  - **Limits:** the abstract does not say whether promised times or cancellations are included; delivery, not passengers; 2015.

### 7. Published asymmetry results (Harter et al. 2025; Mao et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2025)

- **What they offer:** they justify weighting lateness against a promise more heavily than earliness, and measuring against the communicated time.
- **Limits:** I saw only abstracts and quote no effect sizes. The thesis would need the full papers before using any number.
