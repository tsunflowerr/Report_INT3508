**Pattern.**
- **Labels.** Every system learns from observed outcomes of past trips: trip or segment times, or a reliable/not-reliable label.
- **Historical profile.** Our historical time-of-day profile is the kind of input these models use, and the kind of baseline they beat. Google feeds 17-week time-of-day averages to its GNN and uses a 17-week hour-of-day × day-of-week average as a baseline (E4 p.3, p.5). Schilde et al. plan dynamic DARP with 24 hourly average speeds (P9).
- **Model families.** Tree ensembles were the production workhorse: XGBoost at Uber (E1, E2), gradient-boosted trees at Lyft (E9), and "tree-based ML models" at DoorDash (E13). Uber and DoorDash then moved to deep networks (E1, E2, E13).
- **Retraining.** Uber and Grab retrain weekly (E1 p.6, E10).

## 3. S2: ETAs for pooled rides and demand-responsive transit

1. **Industry quotes a pooling allowance and constrains matching.**
   - UberX Share shows an average add-on: "designed to add 8 minutes … we cannot guarantee arrival times".
   - Uber matches so that "additional pickups won't increase your estimated arrival time" (P4).
   - Uber models the probability of being matched with a co-rider, but for price; nothing on the page says it is used for the ETA (P4).
   - Via reports a mean pickup-ETA error of 24 s in a shared service (P5). How Via produced that ETA is not checked.
2. **Better predictions of pooling-induced changes were an open problem in 2020.** Fielbaum & Alonso-Mora measure changes against "first-announced" times caused by new requests. They state that "there are no public methods to provide these predictions effectively" (P1 fn.7).
3. **The closest work since then.**
   - **Kim et al. (2024): a learned detour buffer.** The offered ETA "includes a buffer for possible detours" and later changes stay "within the previously promised boundary". The slack is chosen by reinforcement learning plus a choice model to maximise revenue (P3). This is a learned promise buffer for pooling, but it optimises revenue, not prediction error.
   - **Wang & Wang (2025): XGBoost for pre-request waiting time** in ride-sharing, trained on 30 million trips (P10). It predicts pickup wait, not drop-off drift caused by pooling.
4. **Learning arrival times that anticipate future requests and dispatch is established outside ride-pooling.**
   - Ulmer & Thomas (2019) estimate service arrival times from simulations of the dispatch policy (P6).
   - Hildebrandt & Ulmer (2022) map state features to expected meal arrival times by supervised learning on simulated policy outcomes (P7).
   - This is the same method family as "learn the pooling-induced drift from simulated history".
5. **Traffic uncertainty in ride-pooling.**
   - Hörl & Zwick (2022) inject travel-time estimation errors into a MOIA-based ride-pooling simulation. The cost shows up as late rejections or delays (P2 p.14).
   - **Their strategy B is close to our rule C.** It allows new pickups only if on-board riders are not dropped later than the minimum under current traffic (P2 p.8). In effect, traffic is excused and extra dispatch delay is forbidden for riders whose constraint would otherwise be violated.
   - **How it differs from our cap.** B applies only when a constraint is violated and has no cumulative budget (my reading of p.8). We must cite it and distinguish it.
6. **DARP.**
   - Stochastic, time-dependent travel speeds have been used for planning (P9), and assignment has been integrated with travel-time predictors (P8).
   - I found no DARP paper that learns the insertion-induced change in a rider's promised time. My searches covered learned insertion costs, stochastic DARP and ML in dynamic DARP. The one ML hit I checked, Bongiovanni et al. (hEART 2020, extended abstract; not in the source list), uses a random forest to predict which destroy/repair operators will improve the objective. It does not predict ETAs.

## 4. S3: distributional promises

- **Asymmetric or quantile point promises are standard.** Uber uses an asymmetric Huber loss (E1 p.6, E2). DoorDash moved from quantile loss to asymmetric MSE, so that "a late delivery is X times worse than an early delivery" (E12). JD.com uses a cost-sensitive quantile-regression-forest rule (D1).
- **Full distributions with a separate decision layer** exist at DoorDash (E13) and DiDi (E7), and in research (D3–D5).
- **Conformal predictive distributions** have been applied to order-fulfilment time, together with a cost-sensitive decision rule (D2).
- **Calibration** is checked with PIT histograms and CRPS (E13), or with empirical coverage (D4).
- **Lyft's approach** estimates P(on time | ETA bracket) and selects an ETA whose reliability exceeds an SLA (E9). That is a quantile promise reached by classification.
- **Matched-cost comparison has industry precedent.** DoorDash reports its 10% tail gain "while maintaining constant average quotes" (E12).

## 5. S4: commitment, consistency and predictability

- **ETA consistency is an industry metric.** DoorDash uses it as a guardrail and unified its models across app stages (E13). Grab wants ETAs "more stable" (E10). Uber's Share matching targets "won't increase your estimated arrival time" (P4). DoorDash and Grab get consistency from the model; none of these sources says it comes from dispatch rules.
- **Industry treats dispatch uncertainty as noise to predict, not to control.** Lyft models driver contention as a cause of unreliability (E9). DoorDash's distribution absorbs "Whether nearby Dashers will accept the order" (E13).
- **The academic framing is the choice our thesis faces:** "better predictions or … make these changes less frequent" (P1 p.10). Stricter stability raises rejections (P2 p.14, citing P1).
- **Learned targets depend on the policy.** Lyft trains on all ETA brackets to avoid "negative feedback loops" (E9). At Uber, an upstream model change caused "calibration drift" downstream (E3). My inference, not a finding of either source: an error model trained under one dispatch rule does not carry over to another.
- **Not found:** a study that measures how a commitment rule changes how learnable promise error is, or that compares "commit" against "predict" at a matched promise length. My queries covered ETA consistency, pooling ETA prediction, follow-ups to P1 and microtransit ETA. Absence from my searches is not proof.

## 6. S5: conclusions

**(a) Standard, so not a contribution**

1. **Learning ETAs from past trip times.** Labels are actual trip or segment times; models run from GBDT to DNN and GNN (E1–E13). Uber and Grab retrain weekly. A historical time-of-day profile is a feature or a baseline in this literature (E4, P9). "We learn from previous trips" is therefore an implementation choice, not a contribution.
2. **Asymmetric, quantile or probabilistic promises.** These use a decision layer, newsvendor-style cost rules, and PIT, CRPS or coverage checks, including conformal methods (E1, E12, E13, D1–D5, E9).
3. **Learning arrival estimates that anticipate future requests or the dispatch policy from simulation** (P6, P7), and learned detour buffers in pooling (P3). "Learn the pooling drift from our logs" belongs to this family; it is not new.
4. **ETA consistency as a goal, and "pickups won't increase your ETA"** (E13, P4). This also falls under the AGENTS.md rule: do not claim ETA limits or consistency as novel.

**(b) What our setting could add (measurement, not a new method)**

5. **A head-to-head test of the two remedies in P1.** Commitment (rule C) against free pooling plus a learned promise, compared at matched mean promise extension. Report late rate against the first promise and two-sided error, and also served and shared counts, since commitment costs service (P2). I found no such comparison.
6. **Separate labels for traffic and dispatch.** The per-rider ledger gives each rider's promise error split into a traffic part and a dispatch part. That lets us report, for each policy, how much of each part can be predicted from the information available when the promise is made. A useful score is the pinball-loss skill of a learned model over the profile. I found no ride-pooling paper that treats dispatch-caused drift as its own learning target.
7. **Prior art to cite and distinguish.** Hörl & Zwick's strategy B (P2 p.8) excuses traffic and forbids extra dispatch delay for at-risk on-board riders. Our cap is cumulative and per rider. Kim et al. (P3) learn a pooling slack for revenue.

**(c) What a fair, small experiment on simulated data needs**

8. **Train and test within one policy, split chronologically by day.** Labels depend on the policy (E3, E9). Never train on one arm and test on another.
   - Compare at matched mean extension, following DoorDash's "constant average quotes" (E12).
   - Baselines: naive, the historical profile, a uniform pad, profile plus pad, and a learned quantile GBDT with and without pooling-state features (load, remaining stops, horizon). These are analogous to the state-dependent estimates of P6 and P7, whose exact features I did not check.
9. **Calibration and outcomes.**
   - Report PIT or coverage at the chosen quantile (E13, D4). A quantile promise means nothing if it is miscalibrated.
   - Report late rate against the first promise, mean and p90 extension, served and shared.
10. **State the expected outcomes in advance.**
    - Under the cap rule, the dispatch part is about 0, and traffic is a single citywide factor [brief]. The learned model should then add about nothing over the profile. That is a sanity check, not a result.
    - The real test is the FREE regime (53% shared, 16.8% more than 60 s late against the first promise [brief]). Does a learned pad reach the cap rule's late rate at equal mean extension while keeping more sharing? Either answer is reportable.
    - The simplified world limits what we can claim: the result bounds how learnable pooling drift is. It says nothing about real-world ETA accuracy.
