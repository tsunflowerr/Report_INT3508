# R13 survey by Codex — independent, 2026-10-10

This report answers O2/O3/O5/O6. All 13 supplied PDFs were extracted and read; the example thesis's evaluation pages were also rendered and inspected. Live primary sources were checked on 2026-10-10. No Claude R13 output was read. No simulation, build, new learner training or source-repository edit was performed. The numerical audit is in [R13-PROPOSALS-CODEX.md](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/codex-work/R13-PROPOSALS-CODEX.md).

Evidence labels matter: **field experiment** identifies the tested intervention's business effect; **stated preference** measures hypothetical choices; **operational model/simulation** evaluates a specified model; **operator disclosure/product terms** establish practice, not causal uplift. A reliable final arrival, a short initial quote, a stable sequence of quotes, and a small dispatch-only ledger are distinct quantities.

## B. Primary business evidence, short verified quotes

**B1 — ride-hailing conversion and return use; field experiments.** Goldszmidt et al., *The Value of Time in the United States*, uses randomized Lyft wait/price treatments. Verified quote: “decrease request rates relative to the control.” Longer waits/prices also affect subsequent app sessions; these experiments vary actual wait and price, not only the displayed quote or dispatch-only changes. Supports a cost to making customers wait and the danger of overly conservative offers; does not identify the value of C. [Author working paper, PDF pp. 21–22 / printed 19–20, §3.2.1 and footnote 29](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/BFI_WP_2020179.pdf).

**B2 — pooled service, compensation and engagement; field experiments.** Cohen, Fiszer and Kim, *Frustration-Based Promotions* (Management Science 68(4), 2022), runs three Via experiments. Quote: “works well for long waiting times but not for long travel times.” Proactive future-use credit improves engagement/profitability in the tested waiting-frustration cases; refund/waiver or apology is not the best alternative. This is unusually relevant **negative** evidence for a drop-time thesis: its travel-frustration treatment did not show the same benefit. It does not validate benefits for 30–120-second dispatch limits. [Authors' complete paper, PDF p. 1, abstract](https://maxccohen.github.io/Frustration-Based-Promotions.pdf), [publisher](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2021.3991).

**B3 — delivery promises and sales/returns/retention; publisher abstract verified, full paper not opened.** Cui et al., *Sooner or Later?*, varies disclosed delivery estimates “while keeping the physical delivery speed unchanged.” The abstract reports faster promises increase sales/profit, but increase returns and reduce retention; a model optimizes lifetime value. This is retail delivery, not minute-scale pooled transport. It rules out “later promises are free because the vehicle arrives at the same time.” [Publisher abstract, methodology/results](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/msom.2021.0174).

**B4 — delivery ETA calibration and stability; operator engineering disclosure.** DoorDash's NextGen ETA work separately optimizes distribution prediction and business decision-making, evaluating CRPS and ETA consistency. Quote: “customers don't see large changes in their ETAs.” Consistency is a guardrail alongside accuracy; the article does not report an identified retention effect of a particular cumulative budget. [DoorDash, Experiment Results](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/improving-etas-with-multi-task-models-deep-learning-and-probabilistic-forecasts/).

**B5 — overpromising versus unattractive long quotes; operator engineering disclosure.** DoorDash's gamma-duration article says overprediction “might result in consumers not placing an order.” It explains costs of both early and late errors and reports improvement to long-tail lateness from distributional modeling. Business rationale, not measured willingness to pay for our C rule. [DoorDash, Significance of Accurate ETA Prediction / conclusion](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/using-gamma-distribution-to-improve-long-tail-event-predictions/).

**B6 — cancellation policy has a monetary connection to ETA drift; product terms.** Uber's Australia/New Zealand driver policy exempts a rider cancellation fee when estimated pickup arrival is “5 minutes or more later than the original ETA.” It also permits cancellation/reassignment for significant ETA/distance increases. This establishes an operational consequence; no causal cancellation-rate reduction for C is published there. Market-specific **pickup** policy. [Uber Help, Rider-initiated cancels](https://help.uber.com/driving-and-delivering/article/a-guide-to-our-cancellation-policy-in-australia-and-new-zealand?nodeId=f02cbea7-cde0-4567-92f2-9bc6b3072029).

**B7 — transit information and rider experience; observational study.** Watkins et al., OneBusAway, observes riders and models perceived wait: quote “decreases the perceived wait time by 0.7 minutes.” Information users also experienced shorter actual waits, but this comparison is not randomized and can reflect changed arrival behavior/self-selection. Better usable information can benefit riders without changing the bus trajectory; it does not prove that suppressing ETA updates benefits them. [Author manuscript, PDF p. 3 abstract](https://nacto.org/wp-content/uploads/10-Watkins-Where-is-my-Bus_2010.pdf).

**B8 — pooled reliability willingness to pay; stated preference.** The supplied Alonso-González paper finds positive reliability valuation and heterogeneity, using planned times and five outcome percentiles from travel/wait distributions. Quote: “the reliability ratio … being around 0.5” (ellipsis omits words). A measure of variability has value relative to mean time; it is not a price estimate for Σ|c|, nor a local revealed-preference result. [PDF 1, text:31](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/codex-work/r13-paper-text/Value_of_time_and_reliability_for_urban_pooled_on-.txt:31), [attribute construction, PDF 3, text:152](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/codex-work/r13-paper-text/Value_of_time_and_reliability_for_urban_pooled_on-.txt:152).

**B9 — operator attribution, not causal proof.** Lyft's August 2026 prepared remarks explicitly refer to “the reliability and consistency that keeps riders coming back,” alongside scheduled-airport growth. This is the operator's interpretation of performance amid many concurrent changes. Do not attribute the growth to its guarantee or translate it into a RideBound revenue coefficient. [Lyft, One year of going global / airport rides](https://www.lyft.com/blog/posts/lyft-q2-2026-earnings-prepared-remarks).

**What remains unverified.** I found no primary causal estimate of complaints/refunds/retention attributable to a per-rider cumulative dispatch-change budget, no customer preference estimate separating dispatch causes from traffic causes, and no WTP threshold selecting 30 versus 120 seconds. The sources support a research motivation and trade-off axes, not a business-case percentage for C.

## C. What researchers already optimize

The supplied **Fielbaum/Alonso-Mora §6** is the closest conceptual prior: accepted passengers cannot subsequently be rejected; a fully controlled variant fixes the latest pickup to its announced time while allowing rematching to a vehicle that can meet it. Its Table 5 raises rejection from 34.5% in base to 45.6% fully controlled, while intermediate has 31.2%; average modeled user cost is 9.56 → 11.6 fully controlled. These are that paper's scenarios, not RideBound measurements. The curve need not be monotone on every quality dimension. [PDF 17, text:975 and 987](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/codex-work/r13-paper-text/Unreliability_in_ridesharing_systems_Measuring_cha.txt:975).

Molenbruch's **78 service-level combinations**, Kovacs's consistency models, and MPS freezing show researchers already study hard limits, penalties and operational efficiency together. Schulz/Pfeiffer additionally connects **relative detour, offer acceptance, time-window width and quick computation**. These sources justify optimizing a reliability constraint/frontier. They do not make our metric novel by themselves; see table A's primary-document anchors.

A fresh **2026 preprint** by Schmidt, Golak, Schulz and Fliedner models service commitments, demand and expected revenue in a common-destination shuttle. Quote: “An ambitious commitment attracts higher demand than a conservative one.” It optimizes an offered arrival commitment and can reject accepted customers in stage two. Its revenue/service trade-off is **model-derived**; the assumed acceptance response is not an empirical rider reaction to C. [Full primary preprint, PDF p. 1 abstract / pp. 1–2 introduction](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11950).

For algorithm optimization, Gschwind/Drexl's 2019 DARP work already has an “exact amortized constant-time algorithm” for insertion feasibility. Its stated model includes capacity, windows, route duration and ride times; applicability to RideBound's travel contract, frozen prefix and commitment ledger is not automatic. I opened the publisher abstract, not a new full-paper audit. [Publisher abstract](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/trsc.2018.0837); [local audited applicability boundary, §4](C:/Code/RideBound-noworse/docs/03-related-work-and-claim-boundary.md:95).

**Narrow contribution still defensible:** a cause-separated, path-dependent per-rider cumulative dispatch budget with reproducible certification, then its measured coverage/reliability/quote-cost trade-off. No “first stability control,” “first reliability objective,” “first forecast ETA” or “first time-consistent DARP” claim.

The exact current dictionaries are verified; **actual Parquet timestamp storage resolution, timestamp granularity in data, missingness and target-year schemas are separate checks still needed before training**. The TLC page explicitly warns that reported records are not guaranteed accurate and detailed HVFHV is a separate dataset from 2019. Its monthly links were independently parsed from freshly downloaded HTML ([saved page](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/codex-work/R13-public-nyc-tlc-page.txt)); quote: “Since 2019 high volume trip records (HVFHV) are reported in a separate (and more detailed) dataset.” [TLC primary download page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/tlc/about/tlc-trip-record-data.page).

**Feature leakage boundary.** `shared_request_flag`/`shared_trip_authorized` encode requested product intent. Actual `shared_match_flag`, `trips_pooled`, final `trip_time`, realized distance/fare, or drop timestamp describe later outcomes: useful targets/strata, not prequote features unless availability is independently established. `on_scene_datetime` is also later than an initial offer. Public completed-trip records lack offer histories, unpublished fleet alternatives, routes/co-rider identities and the C policy. These absence/availability conclusions follow from the published schema definitions; they do not claim access to private provider data.

**A legitimate auxiliary ML question:** do chronological OD/time-of-week quantile-duration models trained over several earlier weeks improve future *completed-trip duration* prediction/calibration over a simple weekday/time-bin median/quantile? That outcome is not automatically the simulator's city-wide multiplicative traffic factor: dwell, route choices, pooling and demand mix contaminate it. Converting to a factor requires a pinned routing baseline and an independent transfer validation. Prefer New York for geography/year compatibility; Chicago is useful external duration data with a coarse clock, not a direct Manhattan factor replacement. Fit earlier weeks, calibrate on subsequent weeks, test later weeks; deduplicate and report day-level errors, sharing intent/match coverage and subgroup calibration.

**Recommendation for O5:** yes, more public trip data exists; no, extra trip counts do not automatically supply the missing promise-error or C-causality labels. If using HVFHV, make schema/resolution/missingness validation the first gate. The API 403s and the lack of actual row inspection are documented, not silently filled in from memory.
