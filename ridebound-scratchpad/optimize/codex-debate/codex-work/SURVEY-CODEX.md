## S1. Pooling rate and scale

**Our sharing rate is low relative to one large NYC simulation, but the same-release FleetPy paper does not report a comparable 90% sharing rate.** Service coverage, vehicle occupancy, potential shareability, and realized shared-rider fraction have different denominators.

| Study | Demand / fleet | Wait and detour constraints | Relevant result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engelhardt et al., FleetPy (2026), same Zenodo release | 21,400 requests on 12 November 2018, from a 10% sample; 180 capacity-four vehicles | Maximum wait 480 s; relative detour 40%; 60 s reoptimization; 30 s boarding/alighting | 90% **service coverage**; 1.33 average occupancy across all vehicles. Neither is a shared-rider fraction. |
| Alonso-Mora et al. (2017), different NYC vintage | May 5–11, 2013; 382,779–460,700 requests/day; fleets of 1,000–3,000 | Capacities 1/2/4/10; maximum wait 120/300/420 s; total additional journey delay, including waiting, capped at twice maximum wait | Approximately 90% realized shared rides in the paper's summary; the fraction varies with settings. |
| Santi et al. (2014), 2011 NYC | Over 150 million taxi trips; matching analysis rather than an operational fleet comparison | One-minute online matching horizon; pickup/drop delay tolerance Δ relative to individual service | At Δ = one minute, online shareability below 30%, versus 94.5% oracle shareability; increasing Δ increases feasible matching. |

Evidence for the table:

- [FleetPy, DOI 10.1186/s12544-026-00823-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12544-026-00823-3), §§4–4.1: “based on a 10% sample of trip requests” and “average occupancy rate for all vehicles”. **Data availability** identifies Zenodo 15187906; Fig. 7C shows occupancy, not the requested passenger denominator.
- [Alonso-Mora, corrected author copy, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1611675114](https://autonomousrobots.nl/assets/files/publications/17-alonsomora-ridesharing-pnas.pdf), pp.465–467, Fig.5E: “about 90% of the rides were shared”. Its driver discussion says “Lower fleet size, larger capacity and longer waiting/delay times” (p.466). The shared denominator is picked-up passengers, making this closer to our metric.
- [Santi, author copy, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1403657111](https://senseable.mit.edu/papers/pdf/20140701_Santi_etal_QuantifyingBenefits_PNAS.pdf), Results, p.13292: “the Online model only less than 30%”. Its demand experiment describes “reach saturation and near-maximum shareability” around 100,000 trips/day (Discussion, p.13294). That threshold concerns potential matching in its geography and model, not a universal pooling threshold.

Our **22.8% under U** counts completed riders with positive same-vehicle onboard overlap ([pooling-share output:2](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/pooling-share-2026-10-06.txt:2); definition in [pooling_share.py:20](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/pooling_share.py:20)). Only **7.1%** have a recorded nonzero dispatch-induced drop revision ([exposure output:2](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/exposure-check-2026-10-06.txt:2)). Sharing can be planned before the first promise, can produce zero ETA change, or can shorten a projection. Therefore sharing is not synonymous with later pooling-induced deterioration; this exposure measure includes advances and is not an insertion count.

A representative fixture has **108 target requests, eight capacity-four vehicles, a 96-node service-pool cap, ten-minute pickup allowance, and 1.5× direct ride-time constraint**. Its source demand window is **two hours**, not ten minutes ([configuration:1](C:/RideBoundData/research/fixtures-panelA-v1/d20181114-s10-r3-w08/normalizer-configuration.json:1)). Selected requests have shorter base-graph direct paths than eligible requests: median **165 versus 196 seconds**, with the selected median lower in **49/49** fixtures ([selection check:2–4](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/selection-length-check-2026-10-06.txt:2)). These are repeated fixture appearances, not unique citywide riders; the output's “free-flow” label is not independently verified here.

**Interpretation:** local request density, trip duration, fleet availability, geometry, assignment method, dwell assumptions, and constraints plausibly explain the gap. Total scale alone does not identify their causal ranking. A longer pickup allowance does not compensate automatically for shorter onboard overlap opportunities. Our selected insertion experiment is not a miniature reproduction of a full-city assignment study.

Our algorithm is a **historical-average baseline**, not a new learned traffic predictor. Let `m_b` average the source days' absolute factors. For a naive promise `P` at time `t`, remaining work is `W=P−t`; solve

`∫[t,t+Δ] 1/g(s) ds = W`, with current-bin `g=1`, future-bin `g=m_b/m_current`, then publish `D=max(P,t+Δ)`.

It crosses piecewise bins and never shortens the naive promise ([promise_stretch.py:3–11](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/promise_stretch.py:3); [display_layer.py:60](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/display_layer.py:60)). The causal rule uses prior same-kind days, excludes 15 November as a source, requires at least two source days, and otherwise displays the naive promise ([display_layer.py:10–16](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/display_layer.py:10)). Leave-one-out rules can include future days and are sensitivity analyses, not deployable forecasts. Treating the entire remaining promise horizon as travel work is a modeling approximation: it includes any remaining planned waiting or service components instead of forecasting individual road legs.

The traffic input is **empirically calibrated Manhattan-wide factors derived from recorded TLC trip durations**. The [dataset README, “Network”, Zenodo 15187906](https://zenodo.org/records/15187906) says “recorded travel times in the NYC TLC trip record data” and “new travel time factor every 15 minutes” ([local README:28,34](E:/RideBoundData/wp6/extracted/sha256/d9/d9e86f33645e5eec287d387f8d63ad41ddf41d4ef648138b65d636482e2c599e/FleetPy_Manhattan/README_Manhattan.md:28)). Our movements remain simulated using a common network multiplier; this is not measured traffic on every edge or reconstructed vehicle trajectories. The current anchored world preserves fixture edge times at its start ([world_v1.py:103](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/tang3/world/world_v1.py:103)); the representative fixture uses a midnight factor ([configuration:1](C:/RideBoundData/research/fixtures-panelA-v1/d20181114-s10-r3-w08/normalizer-configuration.json:1)). Anchor choice therefore needs sensitivity checking.

**Contribution boundary:** Family A changes displayed promises while holding vehicle outcomes fixed. Its “anticipated” quantity is defined as `D0−P0`; the remaining traffic/accounting residual depends on that choice ([forecast_family_a.py:134](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/forecast_family_a.py:134)). This is forecast-relative accounting, not unique causal identification. A defensible candidate is a reproducible optimization/replay tied to per-rider cumulative, multidimensional revision ledgers and certificates. This survey does not establish global novelty of that combination. Forecasting, padding, or integration alone cannot carry the novelty claim.

## S3. Combining operator-caused and exogenous delay

**Responsibility exclusions and experienced reliability are different quantities.** [49 CFR §37.131(f)(3)(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-A/part-37/subpart-F/section-37.131) excludes, when assessing prohibited paratransit patterns, “traffic conditions affecting all vehicular traffic that were not anticipated”. [Appendix D, commentary to §37.131](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-A/part-37/appendix-Appendix%20D%20to%20Part%2037) rejects “scheduling practices fail to take into account regularly occurring traffic conditions”. These provisions concern complementary paratransit, not a legal requirement for this taxi experiment. The methodological implication is to plan for predictable congestion instead of excusing all traffic.

[FTA Circular C4710.1, §8.5.3, printed pp.8-20–8-21](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/docs/Final_FTA_ADA_Circular_C_4710.1.pdf) measures pickups “within the established pickup window”. The [Amazon Compute SLA, “Amazon Compute SLA Exclusions”](https://aws.amazon.com/compute/sla/) excludes failures “caused by factors outside of our reasonable control”. Such exclusions define contractual responsibility; they do not eliminate what the customer experienced.

Robust planning is also established. [Campos, Munari and Coelho, CIRRELT-2022-34, §2.1.1, p.4](https://www.cirrelt.ca/documentstravail/cirrelt-2022-34.pdf) require “all time windows are satisfied for any combination of Γ arcs attaining their worst-case travel times”. Their uncertainty budget bounds modeled disturbances, unlike an observed revision ledger. [Chassaing et al., G-2016-120, §1, p.1](https://www.gerad.ca/papers/G-2016-120.pdf) define robustness through “the probability to satisfy the time windows constraints”. This supports probabilistic reliability evaluation, not a claim that our implementation already solves chance constraints or guarantees performance under unlimited congestion.

Physical slack has costs. [Liu and Wirasinghe (2001), Introduction, pp.145–146, DOI 10.1002/atr.5670350206](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/atr.5670350206) discuss “slack time allocated to each time point by minimizing the total cost”. [Brueckner, Czerny and Gaggero (2021), §1, p.2, DOI 10.1016/j.tre.2021.102333](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/91194/1/Brueckner_Airline_mitigation_propagated.pdf) state “a longer flight buffer reduces the chance of late arrival”; their model includes operating and earliness costs. Display padding and physical recovery capacity therefore need separate scores.

Our algebra resolves question 3: **signed** `Σ(c+x)` is displacement from the first promise and reproduces a one-sided deadline; **absolute** `Σ|c+x|` is the existing V budget. `Σ(|c|+|x|)` is stricter, whereas `Σmax(0,c+x)` irreversibly charges deterioration. These are different contracts. Combining causes is meaningful only after specifying this aggregation, evaluation epochs, and recovery behavior. IDEAS gives the implementation references and forecast-anchor qualifications.

## S4. Optimization levers and measurable trade-offs

Promise length has a business cost, but we have no measured RideBound conversion curve. [Goldszmidt et al., Lyft field experiments (2020), §3, printed p.17](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/BFI_WP_2020179.pdf) vary “both the realized wait time and price”. Higher-wait treatments “decrease request rates relative to the control” (§3.2.1, p.19). This is evidence about pickup waiting and purchase decisions, not an identified effect of padding pooled drop promises.

A cleaner display-only precedent is [Cui et al., “Sooner or Later?”, DOI 10.1287/msom.2021.0174](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/msom.2021.0174), abstract **[abstract only]**: “while keeping the physical delivery speed unchanged”. Faster retail promises raise purchases but worsen returns/retention; the authors optimize customer lifetime value. Transfer of those estimates to ride-pooling would be an untested assumption. [Ray and Jewkes (2004), DOI 10.1016/S0377-2217(02)00655-0, author-posted abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=955454) **[abstract only]** already seek to “maximize profits by selecting an optimal guaranteed delivery time”. Quote optimization itself is not new.

[DoorDash, “Using Gamma Distribution…”, “Significance of accurate long-tail predictions”](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/using-gamma-distribution-to-improve-long-tail-event-predictions/) warns that overprediction “might result in consumers not placing an order”. Its asymmetric error treatment and Uber's quantile ETA precedent in S2 motivate a **lateness–earliness–extension** curve rather than optimizing lateness alone.

Pooling reserves also have precedent. [Schulz and Pfeiffer (2026), DOI 10.1007/s00291-026-00847-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00291-026-00847-0), §6.3.5, say “the effect quickly diminishes as the time shift increases”. Increasing permissible shifts gives more flexibility to serve future requests; excessive shifts hurt the modeled offer's attractiveness. Their numerical optimum and assumed acceptance function are not transferable estimates for our riders. Wait/detour/fleet tuning likewise uses the established knobs documented in S1.

Pareto optimization is established even for service consistency: [Tellez et al., DOI 10.1002/net.22063](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/net.22063), abstract **[abstract only]**, use “an epsilon-constraint approach to illustrate the trade-off”. This concerns consistency across a planning horizon, not precisely our within-trip ledger.

**Recommended thesis framing:** optimize a finite cap or promise rule, report a reproducible frontier, and compare contracts at matched service and promise cost. Measure completed/requested, actual lateness, visible variation, causal revision cost, overrides, sharing, and vehicle work separately. Report common-rider comparisons alongside whole-policy outcomes; served populations can change. Without a demand/price model, optimize these measured operational quantities and show business-weight sensitivity rather than inventing profits. Dynamic insertion, ETA limits, reassignment, least commitment, satisfaction, and generic multiobjective optimization remain outside the novelty claim ([local claim boundary:5,175](C:/Code/RideBound-noworse/docs/03-related-work-and-claim-boundary.md:5)).
