# Historical-trip learning, pooled ETA and commitment

2026-10-08. Primary sources checked below; quotations total at most 25 words per source. Findings describe the inspected evidence, not a claim to have exhausted proprietary systems.

**Trip-trained ETA, traffic prediction and historical profiles are compatible.** The owner is right that completed trips provide training labels. Industry also derives historical traffic features from those observations and combines them with live conditions. Our narrow question is whether controlling future dispatch changes improves the reliability/cost frontier of a frozen predictor, rather than inventing historical-trip prediction.

Historical training must recreate information available at prediction time; a future completed trip supplies a label, not an input. Original taxi durations are not ground truth for a simulated pooled rider whose route, stops and acceptance policy differ. Training on RideBound completions would learn simulator-and-policy-specific outcomes, even when traffic factors are derived from measured trips.

## Pooled rides and insertion/detour uncertainty

**A directly relevant learned pooled-offer model already exists.** [Ding, Dandl and Bogenberger, “Predicting Offers for Mobility-on-Demand Services”](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1765899/h05lm74hek2rv3v75ixnj1tr9.pdf), submitted November 2024, pp.6–8 and 21, explicitly models new requests revising an earlier rider's pickup/drop offer. Its training labels are completed FleetPy services: “The realized services RS provided for each request by fleetpy are documented as target values”. Request information and current fleet routing/position/load state are encoded through pickup likelihood, capacity and detour degree; a CNN encoder and task decoders predict serviceability, waiting and driving time. Consequently, learning realized pooled outcomes from offer-time fleet state is prior art, including dynamic insertion effects. These are simulated labels using taxi-derived demand, not measured pooled customer outcomes. Its case-study conclusions report better driving-time prediction than the insertion heuristic but worse waiting-time prediction; the result does not establish universal superiority or a cumulative promise contract.

Current candidate-insertion delay and realized future insertion delay are different targets. A feasibility calculation can evaluate a known candidate against today's schedule; the initial rider's final arrival also depends on requests and reoptimization that have not occurred. Training inputs must not include those realized future changes. Likewise, predicting future demand for anticipatory dispatch is not evidence of an individual pooled-ETA model.

**Dispatch choices and predictability have an established link.** [Fielbaum and Alonso-Mora, TR-C 2020](https://autonomousrobots.nl/assets/files/publications/20-Fielbaum-TRC.pdf), abstract p.1 and §§1–2, separates changes within a trip from variation across repeated trips and studies assignment-rule reliability costs: “Algorithmic changes to reduce this uncertainty induce a trade-off with respect to the overall quality of service”. This supports the commitment–service trade-off, not a theorem about ML learnability.

[Leonardi, Torrisi, Araldo and Ignaccolo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.11899), 2024 author preprint, §§3.1–3.3 and 5.3, PDF pp.8–10 and 21–25, is even closer to the proposed simple-input pilot. It learns onboard travel time from prior simulated DRT trips, including a direct-distance-only linear regressor with a stretched prediction window. Operational detour/wait/walk settings and fleet dimensioning change window reliability and served demand: “the level of predictability of DRT can be seen as a design parameter of the service” (PDF p.25). These papers establish the general prediction/control idea; their windows and operating constraints are different from our cumulative per-rider revision budget.

## What remains to exploit

**Candidate contribution, not established global novelty:** expose and enforce the dispatch component of a rider's evolving promise, then evaluate what historical-trip prediction can and cannot compensate at a matched quote cost. Industry residual learning, uncertainty-aware estimates and consistency controls already exist. The more specific combination to investigate is a path-dependent controllable C budget, signed dispatch/traffic/execution accounting, and an independently frozen historical-trip predictor. The accounting categories depend on the recorded projection protocol; they are not automatically unique physical causal attributions. The repository already limits its claim to cumulative multidimensional ledgers and machine-checkable certificates ([claim boundary:175](C:/Code/RideBound-noworse/docs/03-related-work-and-claim-boundary.md:175)); this survey cannot certify that nobody else implements the full combination.

The verified identity is A−P0=Σc+Σx+z; compliant C histories constrain Σ|c|, while z is arrival minus last projection ([independent verification:15](E:/Code/Report_INT3508/ridebound-scratchpad/optimize/codex-debate/codex-work/ROUND9-CODEX.md:15)). Therefore a cap may reduce an outcome tail mechanically without making conditional error more learnable or increasing the marginal value of ML. Even static runs contain reprojection x, and a C-30 error is not exactly pure traffic. A possible bound adds the dispatch budget to a statistically calibrated forecast of the remaining residual, validated under the same deployed policy, quote-time forecast and served cohort. A generic ETA quantile or cost-rescaled score does not provide this bound; statistical coverage still needs justified assumptions.

The pilot should test whether simple, quote-time features predict useful residual structure beyond a constant or historical profile, and whether gating dispatch improves the resulting reliability–extension–service frontier. Report chronological, grouped empirical performance and common-rider comparisons; retain whole-policy coverage and sharing costs. Calling this a new ETA learner, asserting that commitment universally makes error learnable, or claiming real-world gains from simulator labels would exceed the evidence. A null result remains useful: prediction may add little because the existing profile captures this world's traffic, while commitment supplies the principal control over later changes.
