2.1.4. Residual slack after initial assignment
    We have defined the type of unreliability this study aims to address, and we now identify another trip characteristic that enables

such unreliability to occur. It is quite likely that, at the time of the assignment of a passenger to a vehicle, the initial values of
travel-time components do not fully utilise the maximum waiting time and maximum delay time. Therefore, the remaining capac-
ity of maximum waiting/delay time can be used for future matching with new passengers. We refer to this remaining capacity as
residual slack after initial assignment, defined as the difference between the maximum waiting/delay time and the initial value
of waiting/delay time. For simplicity, we refer to this quantity as slack in the remainder of the paper. Slack is the source that allows
future changes, i.e., unreliability in travel time, and is defined by Eq. (2).

2.4. Utilisation of the shareability score

    We now know how to compute shareability score  for every passenger at the moment they are assigned. In what follows, we
propose two methods to utilise the shareability score in order to favour the reliability of the system. If we are to reduce unreliability
using shareability scores, we first need to define a metric by which we study the unreliability. The primary metric to study unreliability
is whether each passenger did or did not face any changes at all, and calculate the percentage of passengers who faced a change
throughout their trip. This is because users primarily value trips without any unexpected changes, as this ensures a reliable estimated
time of arrival. Therefore, the first change is a key aspect in the study of unreliability (DfT, 2021; Concas and Kolpakov, 2009).
Accordingly, throughout this paper, we focus on the first change, as it enables a clear distinction between reliable and unreliable
trips.

2.4.1. Slack management
    The primary usage of the shareability score is to directly reduce the unreliability of the system, without significantly affecting its

efficiency. As discussed in Section 2.1.4, the main source of unreliability is the slack that exists for each passenger after the initial
assignment. Therefore, slack management is the key to controlling unreliability. Having passengers with a zero value of slack would
ensure that those passengers would not face a change (the primary metric of unreliability in this paper) because it will not allow
additional travel time, but this would be at the cost of increased rejection rate (the primary metric of inefficiency in this paper), as
explained and shown in Fig. 3 and Fielbaum and Alonso-Mora (2020). This means that there is a trade-off between the unreliability
and the inefficiency of ridepooling systems. Therefore, we must attempt to reduce unreliability while also not increasing inefficiency.
In other words, reliability and efficiency are two conflicting objectives in ridepooling systems, and we should aim to take both of
them into account when we try to improve one of them.

    To study the trade-off between unreliability and inefficiency, we construct policy curves using two different slack management
policies. Each curve is generated by varying the level of unreliability management within a given policy, resulting in different opera-
tional rules and different points along the curve. In the first policy, which acts as our benchmark, the trade-off between unreliability
and rejection rate is explored by directly manipulating the slack uniformly across passengers; in the second, we use the shareability
score to manage slack in a more targeted way. Specifically:

 � Ordinary Control. In this model, we do not use the shareability scores. The ordinary control curve in this scenario is created
    by allocating the same percentage of the slack value to all passengers, and changing the percentage from 0% to 100% to induce
    different conditions, i.e., different points on the curve. The smaller the slack, the greater the reliability but the lower the efficiency.
    This will be used as a benchmark to study the benefits of our model. It is worth mentioning that this benchmark is also related
    to how previous papers studied unreliability. In particular, previous studies have addressed unreliability by applying uniform
    controls across all passengers. For example, Fielbaum and Alonso-Mora (2020) directly prohibit further changes for all passengers
    to eliminate unreliability, while analytical approaches such as Daganzo et al. (2020) and Wang et al. (2021) adjust system-wide
    constraints (e.g., maximum delay or detour) to study their impact on matching rates.

 � Shadow Control. This policy leverages the shareability scores. In this scenario, passengers with low shareability scores are given
    0% of their slack values, and passengers with high shareability scores are allowed to use 100% of their slack values. The different
    points on the shadow control curve are obtained via the threshold that distinguishes between high and low shareability scores. This
    is done by sorting the passengers in ascending order based on their shareability scores, and marking the first % of passengers as
    low probability, with  varying from 0% to 100%. In this context, the threshold is defined in a percentile-based (relative) manner.

    It is worth mentioning that drastic slack management in the shadow policy (allocating either 0% or 100% of the slack) is due
to the fact that, from the user's perspective, the occurrence of the first increase in the travel time is more irritating and frustrating
than the value of that increase, and also than the occurrence or value of subsequent increases (DfT, 2021; Concas and Kolpakov,
2009). Therefore, by setting the slack to zero, we prevent low-score passengers from facing any changes. We remark that this method
provides an additional benefit not captured in the shadow control curve: passengers deemed as low probability ones are given a
guarantee of a fully reliable trip at the time of their request, because of their zero slack.

    In an alternative approach, instead of computing the time-integrated shareability score only at the moment of assignment for
the entire trip, one could update the score by recomputing it at intermediate points during the trip, considering only the remaining
trajectory from that point until the drop-off. This would allow for a more refined and continuous slack management, where decisions
are updated based on the evolving system state. However, such an approach would not align with the objective of this work, which
is to determine the passenger's level of unreliability at the moment of assignment. In particular, updating the score during the trip
would imply that the passenger may be exposed to unreliability before the adjustment takes place, and would also make the assigned
level of reliability subject to change over time11. For these reasons, we compute the score once at the moment of assignment and use
it for the entire trip.

    Furthermore, the choice of assigning zero slack to low-shareability passengers and full slack to high-shareability passengers is
driven by the asymmetric impact on system efficiency. Setting the slack to zero removes future pooling opportunities for a passenger,
and the resulting efficiency loss depends on how likely that passenger is to be matched with future requests. For low-shareability
passengers, this loss is limited, as their pooling potential is already low. In contrast, high-shareability passengers have a much greater
potential for future matches, and restricting their slack would eliminate a significant number of feasible pooling opportunities, leading
to a substantial reduction in efficiency. Therefore, the proposed policy targets slack reduction where it has minimal impact on
efficiency, while preserving flexibility where it is most valuable.

    The proposed approach can be naturally extended to subsequent changes along a passenger's trip. Specifically, while in this work
we adjust the slack immediately after the initial assignment to control the occurrence of the first change, a similar mechanism could
be applied after the first change to control the second change, and so on. However, we focus on the first change as it allows us to
clearly distinguish between fully reliable and unreliable trips. By preventing the first increase in travel time for low-score passengers,
we are able to provide a guarantee of a fixed travel time at the time of assignment. Such a guarantee would not be possible if even a
single change were allowed, as passengers would still face uncertainty regarding whether their travel time may increase.

2.4.2. Informing passengers
    This approach considers a setting in which no operational constraints, such as slack, are modified. Its purpose is to explore

an alternative way of addressing unreliability, where passengers are informed about the uncertainty of their travel time rather than
controlling it through system decisions. In this setting, all passengers remain eligible for future insertions and may experience updates
to their travel time. As a result, system efficiency is fully preserved while reliability is addressed through information provision. We
assume that providing this information does not induce behavioural responses, such as passengers reconsidering their trips.

    To support this objective, the shareability score is used in a different way compared to the slack management approach. Instead
of restricting passengers, the score is used to distinguish between those with higher and lower likelihood of experiencing a change
in travel time, and this information is communicated to them. The passengers classification is similar to Section 2.4.1 but requires a
different implementation: while slack management relies on a static, exogenous threshold to separate low- and high-score passengers-
where the threshold is varied across simulations to generate the policy curves-the informing passengers strategy uses a dynamic,
endogenous threshold that is optimised and updated during the simulation based on recent prediction performance. This ensures
that the classification remains meaningful for informing passengers, as the goal is not to control system behaviour but to provide
accurate and relevant information. In this case, the threshold is defined as an absolute (value-based) cutoff on the shareability score,
as opposed to the percentile-based (relative) threshold used in Section 2.4.1.

    In particular, after the calculation of the shareability score at the time of assignment, we categorise the passenger into one of the
following two groups based on their shareability score: low probability or high probability. This is done by comparing the shareability
score with an endogenous dynamic threshold explained below. If the score is lower than the threshold, the passenger is labelled as
low probability; if it is higher than the threshold, the passenger has high probability.

    However, since demand is inherently stochastic, a fixed threshold is not sufficient to maintain reliable predictions under varying
demand patterns. Therefore, the threshold is optimised and updated over time to adapt to these changes. Once passengers are cate-
gorised, the system is executed-without modifying the assignment algorithm (i.e., without adjusting the slack)- to reveal their actual
trip experiences. Then, the predicted information given to users is compared against their actual experience to assess the accuracy of
the prediction. Based on the predicted probability of facing a change and the actual experience of the trip, each passenger can have
four different prediction outcomes:

 � True Positive is when the passenger is predicted as high probability, and they actually faced a change in their trip.
 � True Negative is when the passenger is predicted as low probability, and they actually did not face a change in their trip.
