# Ride-Pooling Personalised Fares: Detailed Summary

## Problem Setting

The paper addresses pricing in ride-pooling systems where travellers experience discomfort from detours and longer travel times, compensated via discounts. The core challenge is balancing operator profit maximization with traveller acceptance in a heterogeneous population with unknown preferences.

## Traveller Acceptance Modelling

Acceptance is modelled probabilistically rather than as deterministic choice. For each traveller-ride pair, acceptance probability is derived from utility comparison:

**"ℙ(Xi,rl(λi,rl)=1) = ℙ(Ui,rls(λi,rl) - Uins > 0)"**

The utility function incorporates:
- Fare (ρ), personalised discount (λi,rl)
- Travel/wait time increments
- Latent behavioural parameters: value-of-time (βt) and sharing penalty (βs)

Since individual parameters are unknown but population distributions are assumed known, acceptance depends on the probability that the utility difference exceeds zero. For multi-person rides, acceptance requires all co-travellers to accept simultaneously and independently.

## No Menu/Self-Selection

Critically, travellers are **not offered multiple options**. The operator constructs a single "offer"—each traveller receives exactly one ride proposal (shared or private). This is enforced via matching constraints:

**"∑r∈M(D)|T(r)|=|D|" and "∀r∈M(D)t∈T(r)⟹∀tc∈T(r)M(tc)=r"**

All co-travellers in a shared ride receive identical offers or none realises.

## Optimisation Formulation

**Objective:** Maximise expected profitability per kilometre (expected revenue divided by expected distance).

**Local (Ride-Level) Optimisation:**
For each ride rl with k travellers, find personalised discounts λ1,rl,...,λk,rl maximising:

**Γs,rl = γs,rl(λ1,rl,...,λk,rl) / ψs,rl(λ1,rl,...,λk,rl)**

where γs,rl is expected revenue (accounting for all acceptance/rejection scenarios) and ψs,rl is expected distance.

**Global (Matching) Optimisation:**
Integer linear programming selects a subset of the shareability graph (rides to offer) such that each traveller appears exactly once and average expected profitability is maximised. The objective is Γ multiplied by ride degree (to normalise for group size).

## Theoretical Results

**Theorem 1:** Locally optimal pricing is globally optimal. The proof shows that if local pricing contradicted global optimality, there would exist a ride where local and global prices differ, contradicting local optimality itself.

This justifies decomposing the problem into independent per-ride optimisations.

## Algorithm and Complexity

**Three-Stage Approach:**

1. **Shareability Graph Construction:** Use ExMAS algorithm (Kucharski & Cats 2020) with behaviour quantiles (α=0.2) to identify feasible ride combinations.

2. **Discretization:** Reduce continuous discount search space by discretizing behaviour distributions into finite supports. Since acceptance probability is piecewise-constant across discount intervals, only calculate at boundary points (nt × ns values where nt, ns are support sizes).

3. **Ride-Level Pricing:** Exhaustive search over discretized discounts to maximize expected profitability for each ride.

4. **Matching:** Standard integer linear programming (using PuLP library).

Complexity is not explicitly stated, but exponential in number of travellers per ride and polynomial in graph size given discretization bounds.

## Experimental Setup

- **Location:** Manhattan, NYC
- **Demand:** Historic NYC Taxi Commission data; 300 requests/hour, 30-minute batch (~150 travellers)
- **Population:** Four behavioural classes from Alonso-González et al. (2021), with value-of-time means [16.98, 14.02, 26.25, 7.78] $/hr and sharing penalties [1.22, 1.135, 1.049, 1.18]
- **Parameters:** Guaranteed discount λ̂=5%, flat baselines 15%/20%
- **No actual fleet simulation**—analysis conducted on static request batch with precomputed shareability graph

## Main Quantitative Results

| Metric | Personalised | Flat 15% | Flat 20% |
|--------|-------------|----------|----------|
| Avg Expected Profitability | 3.29 | 2.70 | 2.73 |
| Total Expected Distance (km) | 332.20 | 358.11 | 348.06 |
| Private Rides in Offer | 14 of 150 | 28 of 150 | 33 of 150 |
| Avg Ride Degree | 2.23 | 1.91 | 2.05 |

**Key findings:**
- Mileage reduction: 4.5% versus flat 20% baseline
- Profitability improvement: 20% over flat baseline
- Personalised pricing assigns low discounts to inefficient rides, high discounts to system-critical ones
- Acceptance probability for efficient rides (>30% distance saved): >80%

## Key Related Work Positioning

The authors contrast their approach against:

- **Flat pricing studies** (Pandit et al. 2019, Li et al. 2022, Ke et al. 2020): assume homogeneous populations, ignore heterogeneity
- **Discomfort-proportional pricing** (Karaenke et al. 2023): ex-post personalisation but no system-level differentiation
- **Matching algorithms** (Alonso-Mora et al. 2017, Bilali et al. 2020, Ke et al. 2021): optimise matching given fixed fares
- **Simplified heterogeneity** (Jacob & Roet-Green 2021): only two traveller types

Their contribution: first to jointly optimize pricing *and* matching under continuous heterogeneity in a probabilistic framework that reconciles operator profit with traveller satisfaction.

**Citation:** arXiv:1811.08238 [cs.DS], submitted November 20, 2018

**Authors:** Lin Chen, Franziska Eberle, Nicole Megow, Kevin Schewior, Cliff Stein

## Abstract (Verbatim)
"We study a fundamental online job admission problem where jobs with deadlines arrive online over time at their release dates, and the task is to determine a preemptive single-server schedule which maximizes the number of jobs that complete on time."

## Commitment Models
The paper examines three commitment frameworks:
1. **No commitment** (baseline)
2. **Delta-commitment:** "commitment decisions must be made before a job's slack becomes less than a δ-fraction of its size"
3. **Immediate commitment:** commitment required upon job start

## Competitive Ratios Achieved
- **No commitment:** O(1/ε) — described as optimal for deterministic algorithms
- **Delta-commitment:** O(ε/((ε−δ)δ²)) for 0 < δ < ε
- **Immediate commitment:** O(1/ε²)

## Impossibility Result
"For scheduling with commitment the restriction to the 'unweighted' throughput model is essential; if jobs have individual weights, we rule out competitive deterministic algorithms."

## Key Assumption
The analysis assumes slackness: feasible scheduling windows are at least (1+ε) times processing time for ε > 0.

# Formal Definitions and Results from "A General Framework for Handling Commitment in Online Throughput Maximization"

## Commitment Models

**No Commitment:** Jobs may be discarded at any time before completion.

**Commitment Upon Job Admission:** "an algorithm may discard a job any time before its start, its admission."

**δ-Commitment:** An algorithm must commit to complete a job when its slack reduces to a δ-fraction of its original size. "the latest time for committing to job j is d_j−(1+δ)p_j."

**Commitment Upon Arrival:** Algorithms must decide at a job's release date whether to complete it, with no later opportunity to reject.

## Impossibility Results

**Theorem 7 (Commitment Upon Arrival):** "No randomized online algorithm has a bounded competitive ratio for commitment upon arrival."

The proof uses an adversarial instance releasing k waves sequentially. Wave i contains 2^i jobs with processing time (1/2^i)·(1−γ)/(1+ε) and deadline 1. An algorithm committing at release cannot defer decisions, forcing it to complete only one job total while optimal schedules complete ⌈k+1⌉ jobs.

**Theorem 8 (δ-Commitment with Weights):** For any δ>0 and ε with δ≤ε<1+δ, "no deterministic online algorithm has a bounded competitive ratio" for weighted jobs.

## Upper Bound Theorems

**Theorem 1 (No Commitment):** With parameters α=1, β=ε/4, δ=ε/2, the region algorithm achieves "Θ(1/ε)-competitive" ratio for unweighted throughput.

**Theorem 2 (Commitment Models):** With α=8/δ, β=δ/4, the algorithm achieves "O(ε/((ε−δ)δ²))-competitive" for δ-commitment and "O(1/ε²)" for commitment upon admission.

## Algorithmic Technique: Region Algorithm

The algorithm maintains two parameters (α≥1, 0<β<1) controlling region sizes and preemption thresholds.

**Core Rules:**
1. "A running job can be preempted only by significantly smaller jobs (parameter β)"
2. "A job cannot start for the first time when its remaining slack is too small (constant δ)"
3. Jobs taking responsibility for preempted jobs reserve time intervals (parameter α)

**Admission Decision:** At job release or region endpoint events, if available job i satisfies p_i<βp_k (where k owns current region), admit i and reserve region [t, t+αp_i).

**Scheduling:** "At any time t, run an admitted and not yet completed job with shortest processing time."

## Slackness Assumption

An instance has ε-slack when "every job satisfies d_j−r_j≥(1+ε)p_j" for ε>0. This assumption circumvents impossibility results for tight deadlines where d_j−r_j≈p_j.

## State-Dependent Utility Model

Disutility combines monetary and inconvenience costs. For customer *i* at time *t_j*:

*"𝒟𝒰_i(t_j) = f(i,S_ij) + ℐ𝒞_i(S_ij)"* where *f* is the monetary cost and *ℐ𝒞_i* represents inconvenience from other customers.

In ridesharing: *"ℐ𝒞_i(S,r_S) = α_i(d_i(S;r_S) − d_i({i};r_{i}))"* — detour sensitivity multiplied by extra distance traveled.

---

## Sequential Individual Rationality (SIR)

**Definition 2.3:** *"A cost sharing scheme f is Sequentially Individually Rational (SIR) if 𝒟𝒰_i(t_j) ≤ 𝒟𝒰_i(t_{j−1}) ∀ 1≤j≤n ∀i∈𝒩."*

This requires disutility to be nonincreasing as each new customer arrives—stronger than traditional IR.

---

## Sequential Fairness (SF)

**Definition 2.6:** A budget-balanced SIR scheme is *β⃗-sequentially fair* when the incremental benefit to existing passenger *i* from new arrival *j* is proportional to *i*'s incremental inconvenience cost.

Formally, the ratio of *i*'s benefit to total incremental benefit equals: *"β_j · ℐ𝒞_i / Σℐ𝒞_m"* for existing passengers.

---

## Key Theorems

**Theorem 3.3 (SIR-Feasibility Characterization):** Route is SIR-feasible iff *"α_op(d(S(j))−d(S(j−1))) + Σ_{i=1}^{j−1} α_i(d_i(S(j))−d_i(S(j−1))) ≤ α_op·d({j}) − α_j(d_j(S(j))−d({j}))"* for all *2≤j≤n*.

**Theorems 4.2–4.5 (Starvation Factor Bounds):** Under single-dropoff setting:
- When *α_i/α_op → ∞*: starvation factor = 1
- When *α_i/α_op = 1*: bounds are *Θ(√n)* upper and *Θ(log n)* lower
- When *α_i/α_op → 0*: upper bound is *Θ(2^n)*

**Theorem 5.2 (Sequential Fairness Characterization):** Incoming passenger *j* pays *"β_j[α_op·S_jD] + (1−β_j)[(α_op+Σα_m)(S_{j−1}S_j+S_jD−S_{j−1}D)]"*; existing passengers receive discounts proportional to inconvenience suffered.
