## Commitment Models

1. **No Commitment**: The algorithm can defer scheduling decisions without advance obligation.

2. **Delta-Commitment**: "If the commitment decisions must be made before a job's slack becomes less than a δ-fraction of its size, we prove a competitive ratio of O(ε/((ε−δ)δ²)), for 0<δ<ε."

# Formal Definitions and Main Results

## Formal Definitions

**Consistency and Robustness:** For a minimization problem with algorithm ALG receiving prediction y of unknown parameter x:

- **Consistency:** c = sup_x [ALG(x,x)/OPT(x)]
- **Robustness:** r = sup_{x,y} [ALG(x,y)/OPT(x)]

**Smoothness:** The performance degrades gradually as prediction error increases, avoiding abrupt degradation.

**Brittleness (Definition 1.1):** An algorithm with robustness r is brittle if "∀ε>0: sup_{x,y:|x−y|≤εx} ALG(x,y)/OPT(x) = r"—meaning worst-case performance immediately jumps to robustness bound even with arbitrarily small errors.

## Problems Covered

The paper analyzes three fundamental problems:
1. **Line Search** (finding hidden target on infinite line)
2. **One-Max Search** (online price selection problem)
3. **Ski-Rental** (rent vs. buy decision with unknown season length)

## Key Negative Result on Brittleness

**Main Finding:** The authors demonstrate that Pareto-optimal consistency-robustness tradeoffs in these problems are inherently brittle. For example, for line search's algorithm A^LS_b: "any algorithm achieving a Pareto-optimal tradeoff between consistency and robustness is brittle."

## Pareto-Optimal Tradeoffs (Line Search)

Algorithm A^LS_b achieves:
- Consistency: (b+1)/(b-1)
- Robustness: 1 + 2b²/(b-1)

These are proven Pareto-optimal but brittle.

## Primary Contribution

The paper proposes **randomization as a solution**, introducing parameter ρ≥0 to trade consistency for smoothness while maintaining Pareto-optimal robustness levels—demonstrating that "certain problems involve multiple tradeoffs."

# Online Minimum Cost Matching on the Line with Recourse - Key Results

## Recourse Model Definition
The abstract describes allowing algorithms to "revoke online decisions to some extent," measuring recourse as the total number of reassignments performed throughout the algorithm's execution.

## Main Theorems

**General Result:**
An O(1)-competitive algorithm using at most O(n log n) total reassignments across all requests.

**Alternating Instances Result:**
For instances with at most one request between consecutive servers, a "(1+ε)-competitive algorithm that reassigns any request at most O(ε^-1.001) times." This represents a per-request recourse bound rather than aggregate.

## Recourse Measurement
The paper measures recourse as worst-case per-request reassignments for the alternating case (O(ε^-1.001) reassignments per individual request), and total aggregate reassignments for the general case (O(n log n) across all n requests).

## Context
This work contrasts with prior results: the best deterministic online algorithm achieved Θ(log n) competitiveness without recourse, matching an Ω(log n) lower bound. The new recourse-based approach achieves constant competitiveness—the first non-trivial result for min-cost bipartite matching with recourse.
