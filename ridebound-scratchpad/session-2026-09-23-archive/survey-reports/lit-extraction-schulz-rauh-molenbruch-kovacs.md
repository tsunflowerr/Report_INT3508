## 1. Schulz & Pfeiffer 2026, OR Spectrum 48:541–586

### 1.1 Mechanism: insertion + between-request precomputed single-customer re-assignment (NOT LNS)

**Two insertion possibilities only** (Sect. 4.1, p. 554):
> "We use two insertion possibilities: First, we check whether the new customer can be inserted into a tour k ∈ K directly. Second, we check whether the new customer can be inserted into a tour k ∈ K if another customer is shifted from the tour k into a different tour k′. In order to do this quickly, we use the time between two consecutive requests to precompute possible insertions of customers into different tours. The set of precomputed tours where one customer is missing is denoted by K′. Let further K′_k ⊆ K′ be the subset of precomputed tours that were generated by removing a customer from tour k ∈ K. The insertion procedure will therefore consider an insert into every tour k ∈ K ∪ K′."

**Explicitly not LNS** (Sect. 2.5, p. 547):
> "In Vallée et al. (2019), a reinsertion algorithm tries to move existing customers around to make room for the new customer [...] However, their reinsertion algorithm is based on Large Neighborhood Search while we simply remove a customer from a route and reinsert them into another."

**Precomputation loop** (Sect. 4.2, p. 554):
> "Removing a single already accepted customer and reinsert them into another tour leads to a higher degree of freedom for the insertion of new requests. If a customer is reinserted in a different tour, it is more likely that a new customer can be inserted in the former tour of the reinserted customer. [...] After a customer has been inserted and the system is 'idle' until the next incoming dynamic customer, all possible reinsertions of single customers into other tours are precomputed."

Complexity (Sect. 4.2, p. 554): "The computational effort to evaluate all inserts for a customer into a tour k of length n̄_k is bounded by O(n̄²_k). Since there are n customers, this results in O(n · n̄²_k) [...] the computational effort to evaluate all possible reinsertions in all tours is O(n³) in the worst case."

**What can change after acceptance / what is frozen.**
- Vehicle re-assignment: YES, but only one customer at a time, moved wholesale to another tour (K′ construction, Sect. 4.2).
- Stop re-ordering / re-timing: YES, implicitly — a new insertion pushes back all later stops. The measured consequence is Table 2 "Delay".
- Frozen: (i) the *next* stop once the vehicle is en route — Sect. 3.1 p. 549: "tours are only changed when the vehicle is at a customer location. This means that the vehicle never changes its route while it is driving to a location, but the tour after the next location can be changed"; Appendix C: "Note that we do not change the next stop of a vehicle after it already started driving towards it." (ii) The promised deadline l_{i−}. (iii) Acceptance itself — "customers can never be rejected after acceptance".
- No waiting, no idle-vehicle relocation (Sect. 3.2, Sect. 2.4).

**Feasibility filter** (Sect. 3.2, p. 551) — this is the hard constraint that plays the role of a promise cap:
> "The tour is feasible if i+ is visited before i− (holds for all earlier customers because it was checked when inserting them into s^i_k), no delivery location j− is reached later than the latest possible arrival time l_{j−} for the corresponding customer j and the vehicle capacity Q is never exceeded during the trip."

And it is re-imposed during precomputed reinsertion (Sect. 4.2, p. 554): "In doing so, the time window of the considered customer has to be respected. This can be reached by setting o = l_{i−} + 1 in Line 2 of the algorithm."

Constant-time feasibility test via Forward Time Slack (Appendix B, p. 575–576), eqs. (8) and (9):
> "F_p := min_{p<p′≤n̄} {w_{p,p′} + (l_{σ_{p′}} − B_{σ_{p′}})}"
> (8) "t_{σ_p,m+} + t_{m+,σ_{p+1}} ≤ F_p + t_{σ_p,σ_{p+1}}"
> (9) "t_{σ_{p′},m−} + t_{m−,σ_{p′+1}} ≤ F_{p′} + t_{σ_{p′},σ_{p′+1}} − max{δ − w_{p+1,p′}, 0}"
> "In total, feasibility of an insertion can be checked in constant time with linear effort in a preprocessing step."

### 1.2 The "potential" function — exact definition, computation, use, numerical payoff

**Definition** (Sect. 5.1, p. 556):
> "In order to measure this attractiveness, we define the potential of a set of tours K̄ ⊂ K for a customer b ∈ PC as PT_b^{K̄}. The potential gives the expected acceptance probability for a customer of type b if they were inserted optimally into the best tour k ∈ K̄. To determine the expected acceptance probability we weight the acceptance probability with the probability distribution for the interarrival times, i.e. how likely it is that the customer will appear in a given time interval."

Set of possible customers (Sect. 5, p. 555): "If there are V stops, there are V(V − 1) possible pickup-delivery relations which are all added to the set of possible customer requests PC. [...] In the simplest case, each of the requests b ∈ PC is assumed to be equally likely to occur (with probability 1/|PC|)." In the instances V = 120 ⇒ |PC| = 14,280.

**How computed — three-stage, all in the idle time between requests.**
- Step 1, Algorithm 1 "Precomputation of intervals" (Sect. 5.2.1, p. 557): "A time interval (a1, a2] means that if customer b places their request during the time interval, the optimal insertion position (maximizing customer b's acceptance probability) remains the same." Exploits: "inserting the pickup location b+ of a customer b after a location in the tour σ_i is only possible if the arrival of the customer into the system happens before we leave σ_i, i.e. e_b ≤ B_{σ_i}", and "an optimal insertion position for a request time e_b stays optimal even if e_b is increased, as long as e_b is not greater than the departure time of the location preceding the optimal pickup location of b+."
- Step 2, per-interval expected acceptance, eq. (6) (Sect. 5.2.2, p. 559) — product of acceptance function (2) and exponential interarrival density, integrated over the interval:
> "∫_{a1−T}^{a2−T} exp(−µ · (B_{b−} − (T + y) − t_b)/t_b) · λ · exp(−λy) dy = λ · exp(−µ · (B_{b−} − T − t_b)/t_b) · [exp(−(λ t_b − µ) y / t_b) · t_b/(λ t_b − µ)]_{a1−T}^{a2−T}"
  with the degenerate last interval given separately by eq. (7): "∫ exp(−µ · t_{σ_{n̄},b+}/t_b) · λ · exp(−λy) dy = λ · exp(−µ · t_{σ_{n̄},b+}/t_b) · [−exp(−λy)/λ]".
- Step 3, Algorithms 2 and 3 (Sect. 5.2.2, p. 560): intervals are intersected across tours and the best tour per intersection is taken: "For each intersection, we can then calculate the weighted acceptance probability by using the best acceptance probability across all tours, since we care only about the best possible offer we could make the customer b ∈ PC." Algorithm 3 averages over all b ∈ PC with weight 1/|PC| (or cluster weights (γ_c + γ_{c′}) / (2(χ−1) Σ γ_{c″}), Appendix G).

**The "ruined tour" bookkeeping — the mechanism's core trick** (Sect. 5.2.2, p. 559–560):
> "Let i be a new dynamic customer that arrives into the system and we now want to evaluate the effect on the potential if we were to insert the customer i into tour k = 1. In such a case, the tour k = 1 is 'ruined' and should not be considered when calculating the potential, as we calculated the potential for tour k without the new customer i. [...] We, therefore, assume that if a tour is changed by inserting a customer into it, we lose the potential it had for any possible customers (and is thus ruined). Additionally, if there are precomputed tours K′_1, which are tours that were originally based on tour k = 1, i.e. are tour 1 but with one removed customer, they are also ruined. If we insert into a tour k ∈ K′, we need to consider the fact that another tour will be ruined by the reinsertion of the customer that was removed to create tour k ∈ K′." And K̄ = (K ∪ K′)\K̂.

**How it enters the insertion decision** (Sect. 5.2.3, p. 561, Algorithm 4 "Evaluation of inserts"):
> "Like before, the acceptance probability prob is an important criterion. However, a second part of the objective is now the potential over all tours that are not ruined by the respective insert. The two parts are combined in a weighted sum where 0 ≤ ω ≤ 1 gives the weight of the potential."
Objective (Sect. 5, p. 555): "min_π (1 − ω) · E[C(S_{i−1}, W_i, x^π_i, W̄_i)] + ω · E[C(S_i, W_{i+1}, x^π_{i+1}, W̄_{i+1}) | W̄_i = 1]".
Also used as a tiebreak on where to put the displaced customer (Sect. 5.2.3, p. 561): "If there are multiple tours, we choose the tour that has the least impact on the potential."
Anytime property: "In the case of an early cancellation, already calculated potential values are used normally while all not calculated potential values are treated as zero" (Sect. 5.1); "Should the calculation of the potential be canceled early, the value returned by the CalculateTotalPotential function in Algorithm 3 will be zero" (Sect. 5.2.3).

**What it buys them numerically.** Very little — roughly 0.3 percentage points of rejection rate.
- Table 1 (p. 567), R̄ by ω, aggregated over 16,200 instances each: ω=0.5 → 38.83; 0.7 → 38.87; 0.3 → 38.89; 0.1 → 38.92; 0.9 → 40.69. "It holds for each row that the result is significantly better with p < 0.001 than the result in the next row." Finding 3: "The potential is used best the closer the weighing parameter ω [...] is to 0.5."
- Table 7 (p. 582), all 32 configurations: best C0.5_60 = 38.78%***; C0.5_120 = 38.81%***; benchmark **CB1 (precomputation on, potential off) = 38.92%**; benchmark **CB0 (greedy, no precomputation, no potential) = 39.92%***; worst C0.9_10 = 41.19%***. So potential ≈ 0.11–0.14 pp over CB1; precomputation ≈ 1.00 pp over CB0. Overweighting the potential is actively harmful: all ω=0.9 rows (40.38–41.19%) are worse than CB0.
- Table 3 (p. 570), clustering χ: 120→39.16 (234.53 s); 60→39.19 (63.84 s); 40→39.22 (40.93 s); 30→39.22 (35.26 s); 20→39.26 (32.57 s); 10→39.38 (32.04 s).

### 1.3 Do they ever REVISE a promise already communicated? — NO.

The offer is x_i = (B_{i−}, l_{i−}, k, P_{i−}, D_{i−}) and (Sect. 3.2, p. 551): **"Only B_{i−} and l_{i−} are communicated to the customer."**

The deadline is computed once, at acceptance, and never re-issued (Sect. 3.1, p. 549–550):
> "Although we assume that customers who accepted an offer never reject it later, integration of new dynamic requests should not increase RD arbitrarily for another already accepted customer i. Therefore, we limit the maximum time shift in the arrival time at delivery location B_{i−} to a later point in time by l. We calculate l_{i−} = B_{i−} + l directly after customer i accepted our offer. Together with the request time e_{i+}, customer i has to be transported from their pickup to their delivery location within the time window [e_{i+}, l_{i−}]. The value of l can be communicated to the customer beforehand and is thus included in the acceptance probability. Note that this means that customers can never be rejected after acceptance."

And (Sect. 3.1, p. 550, Fig. 1): "After acceptance the customer can be delayed because of new arriving customers [...] However, l_{i−} = B_{i−} + l sets the deadline for any delay of customer i such that customer i is certainly served within the time interval [e_{i+}, l_{i−}]."

`l` is a **single, customer-independent constant** (Sect. 2.4, p. 546):
> "Our paper includes this aspect by a customer independent time span, which is communicated to the customer and by which we are allowed to delay the customer's arrival time at their delivery location. Haferkamp (2024) uses a function that depends on the direct travel time of a customer to compute the tolerable delay."

Default l = 900 s = 15 min, "which is also done this way in practice" (Sect. 6.2, p. 562).

### 1.4 Acceptance probability: function of the promise at offer time ONLY — YES.

Eq. (1) (Sect. 3.1, p. 549): "RD = (B_{i−} − e_{i+} − t_i)/t_i".
Eq. (2): acceptance probability "e^{−µ·RD}", "where µ > 0 is an additional weight parameter". µ = 0.5 in experiments; "A factor of 0.5 means that a relative detour of 100% is accepted with a probability of roughly 60%" (Sect. 6.2).
B_{i−} is the arrival time proposed at the offer. There is no re-evaluation, no cancellation, no post-acceptance utility update: "we assume that customers never cancel a trip which they have already accepted" (Sect. 2.2); W̄_i ∈ {0,1} is drawn once per request (Sect. 3.2). The only way the future promise leaks into the acceptance decision is the constant l, which is "included in the acceptance probability" but not modelled as a separate argument of (2).

### 1.5 Realized promise slippage — the one number that matters for a "displacement" thesis

Table 2 (Sect. 6.3.4, p. 567–568), "Delay: How much an accepted customer's final delivery time is delayed compared to the original offered delivery time" (seconds):
> CB0 = **181.71**, CB1 = **279.87**, C0.5_120 = **273.20**

> "In Configuration CB0, a customer has to wait roughly three minutes longer until reaching their delivery location compared to the original time they are told. If we enable the precomputation, this value increases by about 1:40 min on average; this is because the precomputation will use the degree of freedom to shift already accepted customers to make room for new customers. When also utilizing the potential (C0.5_120), this effect is slightly reduced, as the interests of future customers are already included to some extent in the initial insert."

Finding 4: "Precomputation and potential do not change the structure of accepted requests, but they are able to use the additional degree of freedom to delay already accepted customers to some extent to offer new customers a good trip."

Sensitivity of rejection rate to the promise budget l (Sect. 6.3.5, p. 568–569; Fig. 7), l ∈ {0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30} min:
> "Expectedly, a larger time shift lowers the rejection rates [...] However, the effect quickly diminishes as the time shift increases. While the difference between l = 0 and l = 5 is fairly large, the difference between l = 10 and l = 15 is already much smaller. This is helpful, since under our assumptions, the time shift l is communicated to the customer beforehand. [...] Note that even for a maximum time shift of 30 min the average fraction of rejected customers does not decrease below 0.38."
Finding 5: "A small maximum time shift leads to significantly higher rejection rates while a larger maximum time shift makes the system unattractive for accepted customers. A reasonable trade-off is to set the maximum time shift to a value of 10 to 15 min."
Also: "Figure 7 specifies the results by adding that there can be a substantial variance in the delay of single customers."

Instance setup (Sect. 6.1–6.2): 120 real virtual stops from a former Hamburg ridepooling provider; average direct travel time ≈ 8.5 min; Q = 6; λ ∈ {0.0111, 0.0167, 0.0333} (1/λ = 90/60/30 s); n ∈ {100,250,500,750,1000}; |K| ∈ {6,8,10}; 900 instances × 3 seeds = 2700; 32 configurations = 86,400 observations.

Future-work note directly relevant to a per-customer promise budget (Sect. 7, p. 573): "the length of the delivery time windows l can be adapted to differentiate between customers, e.g. according to the direct travel time".

## 2. Rauh, Ahrens, Büsing, Comis, Engelhardt 2025, OR Spectrum (DARPCF / MCLIH)

### 2.1 What "flexible scheduling" exactly is, and who holds the flexibility

It is **the GP appointment time that is left open, not the ride window**, and the flexibility is held by **the transport provider** (delegated by patient + GP). Sect. 1:
> "The standard process [...] First, a patient contacts their GP to arrange a fixed-time appointment, e.g., Monday 10 a.m. This time is then communicated to the transportation provider which in turn makes a commitment to take the patient from their home to the appointment and back. While this procedure is relatively comfortable for GPs and patients, it comes with the downside of not synchronizing appointment scheduling and patient transportation."
> "The idea of flexible scheduling is to change the arrangement of appointments and transportation: when a patient arranges an appointment with their GP, only a range for the appointment is fixed, e.g., Monday morning. **The transportation provider can then flexibly schedule and reschedule the patient's rides within the previously agreed range.** Finally, the transportation provider fixes the vehicle routes a few days ahead of transportation and thereby determines the exact appointments which are then communicated to patients and GPs."

Cost borne by the patient, benefit offered in exchange:
> "For patients, this means that the exact appointment times are confirmed closer to the actual appointment date. [...] While flexible scheduling demands some spontaneity from chronic patients, it offers a key benefit: guaranteed transportation to and from appointments."
GP side: "we require the reservation of flexible appointment slots that will only be filled a few days in advance."

Two patient classes: **chronic** = flexible, range-only ("we define these patients as chronic patients"); **walk-in** = "those with fixed-time appointments scheduled on shorter notice", may be rejected: "the transportation provider may turn these requests down if they do not fit into the current vehicle routes."

### 2.2 Is there a negotiated / locked commitment? — Yes, but coarse and one-shot.

Three distinct locks:
1. **The agreed range** is the negotiated object; the exact time is set later by the provider. There is no per-customer ledger of how much the exact time moved inside the range.
2. **All-or-nothing service commitment** (Sect. 1): "Once a transportation request has been accepted, it must be serviced entirely, i.e., neither the outbound nor the inbound journey may be canceled."
3. **The inbound is mechanically chained to the outbound** — Definition 1 (Sect. 3.2): "If an outbound request r_i is scheduled to arrive at time t_i, then the departure time t̄_i of the corresponding inbound request r̄_i must satisfy t̄_i ∈ [t_i + d_GP, t_i + d_GP + W]." So fixing the outbound *creates* the inbound window: "the GP appointments impose a special structure, where fixing one initial outbound ride introduces constraints on the inbound ride" (Sect. 2.3).

**The explicit stability/freezing constraint** is stated only for the online extension (Sect. 3.3, "Further extensions"):
> "• Online requests: Each walk-in request (r^w_i, r̄^w_i) ∈ R_w × R̄_w has a release time τ_i (potentially during the service period), and the operator must either accept and schedule or reject the request. **When accepting the request, the resulting schedule S must remain the same for all stops before τ_i.**"

That is a *prefix-freeze* (nothing already past may move), not a bounded-displacement promise on future stops.

Walk-in windows are hard and tied to the appointment (Sect. 3.3): "[e_i, l_i] = [s_i − W, s_i]" for outbound delivery and "[ē_i, l̄_i] = [s_i + d_GP, s_i + d_GP + W]" for inbound pickup.

### 2.3 Algorithmic mechanism (MCLIH), and what is frozen

Two phases (Sect. 4): "In the first phase, all chronic patient requests are scheduled and in the second phase, all walk-in requests are added."
1. Greedy mini-clustering (Sect. 4.1), Kruskal-like, edge admitted only if "c′_ij ≤ ρ · (t_{i,n+i} + t_{j,n+j})", clusters grown "until their size reaches the vehicle capacity Q".
2. Exact PDP MILP per mini-cluster (Sect. 4.1): "no time windows need to be respected since the mini-clusters only consist of flexible outbound trips."
3. Linking: asymmetric TSP over mini-clusters (ant colony, Dorigo et al. 1996) + optimal split (Beasley 1983 / Prins 2004) under route-duration constraint (1): "Σ_{e∈A(R_k)} c_e + Σ_{i∈V(R_k)} d_i ≤ T_V".
4. Inbound trips inserted by GIH (REBUS-based greedy insertion): "This GIH explores all possible insertions into the existing schedules and greedily chooses the one that minimizes a prespecified metric."
5. Walk-ins inserted online by the same GIH.

Why flexibility helps the clustering metric (Sect. 4.1): "In the classical DARP setting, two requests that lie close to each other are frequently not compatible due to their time windows. Here, outbound trips can be scheduled freely within a time slot and thus this problem disappears."

Only reoptimization hint, and it is hypothetical (Sect. 4.2): "We assume that the number of vehicles is always large enough, such that this insertion is possible. However, it is also an option to remove an outbound request from the schedules in case of an unsuccessful insertion of the corresponding inbound trip and try reinsertion for different time windows." No reassignment/reordering of already-scheduled requests is implemented.

Note also (Sect. 3.2): "the two rides may be scheduled on different vehicles."

In fact, by using MCLIH and the flexible scheduling system, about **38% more requests can be served** compared to the default system using GIH_TW. Moreover, MCLIH gives good solutions consistently, with a standard deviation of 9.5."

## 3. Molenbruch, Braekers, Caris — CEJOR, "Operational effects of service level variations for the DARP"

### 3.1 The two service-level parameters (and nothing else)

Sect. 2: "Each of the 78 quality scenarios considered in this study is a combination of a maximum deviation from the user's preference time and a relative exceedance of the direct ride time. For the first criterion, **a realistic range of 13 settings between 0 and 60 min** is considered. For the second, **a range of 6 settings between 0 and 100 % of the direct ride time** is taken into account."

Parameter 1 = time-window half-width around the stated preference time (Sect. 1): "the width of the time windows at the pickup and delivery locations, which is determined by the allowed deviation from the user's preference time. A user is supposed to indicate either a preferred departure or arrival time."
Parameter 2 = **relative** max ride time, deliberately not absolute (Sect. 1): "this study takes into account the direct ride time of each individual trip. A maximum exceedance of the direct ride time is expressed as a fraction of this direct ride time. Consequently, the ride time for a certain trip is delineated by the sum of the direct ride time and the corresponding maximum exceedance."

Both are **hard constraints inside a static solver** — deterministic annealing (Braekers et al. 2014), 5 runs × 350,000 iterations, initial solution from 1000 parallel-insertion runs; operators: relocate inter-route, exchange inter-route, 2-opt inter-route, r-4-opt intra-route, eliminate. Feasibility = Parragh et al. (2010) eight-step check. Everything can move (static problem, no promises).
Notation convention (Sect. 4.1): "The notation (20, 0.4) refers to the scenario in which the deviation from the user's preference time is at most equal to 20 min, whereas the exceedance of the direct ride time is limited to 40 % of the direct ride time." Baseline = (0, 0.0), "a taxi service for which neither a deviation from the user's preference time, nor an exceedance of the direct ride time is allowed."

### 3.2 Shape of the cost/service-level curve, with numbers

**Convex, with a concave "dead zone" at small ride-time slack** (Sect. 4.1):
> "Except for the range in which the allowed exceedance of the direct ride time is small, the graph exhibits a convex pattern in both directions. This indicates that the marginal benefits of consecutive reductions in the quality level become progressively smaller. In other words, reducing the service quality has a smaller cost effect when the quality level is already poor, indicating that there is no point in performing repeated blind quality reductions to obtain operational savings. In addition, it can be concluded that beyond a certain point, a combined loosening of both quality criteria will bring about larger marginal savings than a unilateral variation in one of them."
> "For limited values of the allowed exceedance of the direct ride time, the influence of the service duration reshapes the graph into a concave pattern. Knowing that it takes a certain number of minutes to board or to leave the vehicle, it is useless to allow a user's direct ride time exceedance smaller than that amount of time. After all, the time margin created is not sufficient to pick up or deliver any other user."

**Anchor points (total distance as % of baseline (0,0.0)):**
- (20, 0.4) = **94.20 %** ("the result in scenario (20, 0.4) shows that the total distance traveled by all vehicles equals 94.20 % of the result in scenario (0, 0.0)").
- (15, 0.4) = **95.60 %** (790.45 km vs 826.87 km baseline).
- Average over all 78 scenarios, all 62 instances = **90.21 %**.
- By provider size (Table 1): smallest (16–32 req, 14 inst) **92.61 %**; small (36–50, 16) **91.47 %**; large (56–72, 16) **90.11 %**; largest (80–144, 16) **88.93 %**. ANOVA F = 13.313, p = 0.000; Levene 0.832 (sig 0.482); Bonferroni-significant pairs: smallest–large (0.001), smallest–largest (0.000), small–largest (0.000).

### 4.1 Every reported "price of consistency" number, verbatim

**Early/implicit (Sect. 4.1, p. 197):**
> "Empirical results indicate that the routing cost of a priori optimization is very close to the cost of reoptimization: Christofides [26] and Golden and Stewart [56] report a difference of **2.7% and 8%**, respectively; Savelsbergh and Goetschalckx [95] give empirical evidence that in some cases a priori solutions even outperform daily routes generated by heuristics; Hemmelmayr et al. [65] compare the a priori strategy to a more flexible routing strategy for an IRP—the differences between the two approaches are small; Bertsimas [12] and Bertsimas et al. **Districting (Sect. 4.2, p. 197):**
> "The increase in travel distance if customers are preassigned to districts compared to daily reoptimization is **3.8%** in Christofides [26] and **7.1%** in Hardy [58]."

**Arrival time consistency (Sect. 5.1.3, p. 203):**

For example, in the ConVRP, a **60% tighter constraint on the maximum arrival time difference increases travel cost by up to 186.16%.** The reason for this result is the large number of routes needed to cope with the strict arrival time difference constraints. **Allowing flexible departure times reduces the impact of tight arrival time consistency constraints on cost significantly, even without idle times along the route. Therefore, arrival time consistency can be improved without increasing working time (Kovacs et al. [73]).**"
> "Francis et al. [50] examine the effect of operational flexibility on arrival time consistency in the PVRP-SC. Arrival time consistency deteriorates by up to **9.8%**, on average, if the number of feasible visit schedules [...] increases from 3 to 10."

> "In Groër et al. For the IRP, Coelho et al. [28] and Coelho and Laporte [31], indicate an average increase between **0.31% and 9.85%** and **0.55%**, respectively. Feillet et al. In Smilowitz et al. **Delivery consistency (Sect. 5.3, p. 207):**

### 4.2 Partial consistency (covering only a fraction of customers) — all evidence

Sect. 5.2.1, p. 204:
> "This constraint is relaxed in Erera et al. [44], Haughton [62], Kovacs et al. [73], Schneider et al. [97], Spliet [101], and Zhong et al. [118]. In **Erera et al. [44], each customer may be visited by two different drivers**; in Haughton [62] and Kovacs et al. [73], **the number of different drivers per customer is a user-defined parameter**. In **Spliet [101], each customer is assigned to one driver in advance; on each day, at least a given fraction of customers has to be visited by the preassigned driver.** In **Schneider et al. [97] and Zhong et al. [118], only a fraction of customers is assigned to the same driver permanently; unassigned customers may be visited by any driver to increase routing flexibility.**"

Sect. 5.2.2, p. 202: "Repoussis et al. [93] present a hybrid solution strategy: Template routes are generated by tabu search. Based on the resulting template, **the customer-to-driver assignment is fixed for a part of the customer set and the partially fixed ConVRP is solved by branch-and-cut**."
Sect. 5.2.2, p. 205 (Erera et al. [44] two-group scheme): "Customers are partitioned into two groups: The first group includes customers that may be visited by any driver (e.g., customers with low probability of placing an order); the second group consists of customers that have to be served by no more than two different drivers [...] Each customer from group two is inserted into a primary a priori route and into a **backup route**. On each day [...] if the demand on a route exceeds the vehicle capacity, **some customers are moved to their backup route**; finally, the partial routes are filled with customers from group one."
Sect. 5.2.2 (Sungur et al. [106] master plan): "The a priori routes, referred to as master plan routes, contain customers with a high probability of placing an order. [...] **Customers are left unassigned if their insertion would violate operational constraints.**"
Sect. 6, p. 208 (partial constraint satisfaction on the inconsistency side): "The time spread constraints are relaxed in Yan et al. [116, 117]. Routes are planned day after day; **a routing plan is feasible if a given fraction of customers is visited outside of the prohibited time windows**."

Quantified payoff of partial consistency: Spliet [101] 75% coverage → 12.7% → 2.9%; Kovacs et al. [73] 2 drivers/customer → up to 6.5% cheaper; Francis et al. [50] <10% of customers stable-quantity → cost down 3.6–4.8%.

### 4.3 Flexible departure times — all evidence

Sect. 5.1.3, p. 203, with Figures 12 and 13:
> "In both papers, the authors require the drivers to start their tour at time zero. Additionally, waiting is prohibited."
> "The left figure shows the solution when departure times are flexible; both customers are visited at exactly the same time on both days they require service. The driver has to start his route at time zero in the right figure. Customer 2 is visited at time one on day 1 and at time 2 on day 3, resulting in an arrival time difference of one time unit."
> "In the left figure, the driver waits one time unit before starting customer 3's service to achieve perfect arrival time consistency. The right figure shows a solution that has a minimal maximum arrival time difference when waiting is prohibited but later departure from the depot is allowed; **by delaying the departure time on day 1 customers 1 and 3 are visited with an arrival time difference of 0.5 time units each. The maximum arrival time difference increases to one time unit at customer 3 if departure time is fixed at zero.**"
> "**Allowing flexible departure times reduces the impact of tight arrival time consistency constraints on cost significantly, even without idle times along the route. Therefore, arrival time consistency can be improved without increasing working time.**"

This is the survey's single most transferable design lesson: a *free* degree of freedom (start-time slack) absorbs most of the price of a tight stability constraint.

### 4.4 Hard constraint vs. soft penalty

Sect. 5.1.1, p. 201:
> "Arrival time consistency is either modeled by hard constraints, that is, variations are bounded by a given value, or soft constraints, that is, variations are penalized in the objective function."
> ConVRP hard version: "In the ConVRP, the maximum arrival time variation is bounded for each customer to a given value L. **The maximum arrival time difference l_max is the largest difference between the latest and the earliest arrival time per customer among all customers.** [...] Arrival time consistency is achieved by enforcing l_max ≤ L. The same modeling approach is applied in Kovacs et al. [74] and Tarantilis et al. [107]. An arrival time difference of l_max = 0 is considered in Ioachim et al. [68]."
> Soft: "Soft constraints for arrival time consistency are examined in Kovacs et al. [73]. The authors penalize the maximum arrival time difference in the objective function because **it is difficult to predict reasonable bounds if demand is fluctuating: loose arrival time constraints decrease service quality while excessively tight constraints lead to a disproportional increase in travel cost.**"
> Decision rule: "**The decision to model arrival time consistency either with hard or with soft constraints depends on the availability of information. Using soft constraints is feasible in both deterministic and stochastic environments. Using hard constraints, however, may lead to infeasible or unreasonable solutions when demand fluctuations cannot be anticipated.**" (Fig. 8 gives the worked counterexample; see (d) below.)

Sect. 5.2.1, p. 204: "**Hard constraints on the number of different drivers per customer have mostly been used in deterministic environments. Fixing the driver-customer assignment in advance in stochastic environments might render routes infeasible when the actual demand of the customers is larger than the vehicle capacity.** Another concern raised by using tight constraints on consistency is the loss of customer satisfaction and driver productivity if the usual driver has to be replaced by an unfamiliar colleague."
Soft person-oriented: "Some authors improve person-oriented consistency by penalizing undesired driver-customer assignments (or rewarding desired assignments) in the objective function (Coelho et al. [28], Coelho and Laporte [30], Eveborn et al. [45], Kunkel and Schwind [76], Schneider et al. [96], Smilowitz et al. [100], Zhong et al. [118])."
Sect. 5.3, p. 207: "Consistency in the quantity delivered or in the frequency of deliveries has **mainly been modeled with hard constraints**."

**A non-max, "regularity-class" alternative to a max-deviation bound** (Sect. 5.1.1, p. 201) — worth knowing for ledger design:
> "For example, visiting a customer on 5 days at (8:00, 8:05, 8:02, 8:58, 9:00) or at (8:00, 9:00, 8:20, 8:40, 8:30) yields the same maximum arrival time difference of 1 h. Yet, the first schedule shows more regularity. Rather than bounding the maximum arrival time difference, Feillet et al. [46] **minimize the maximum number of different time classes each customer is visited in.** Two visits at customer i on day α and β are assigned to the same time class if the arrival times a_iα and a_iβ fulfill |a_iα − a_iβ| ≤ L."

# Direct answers

## (a) Does any paper use a monotone cumulative per-customer ledger of promise revisions, checked as a hard feasibility filter before selection?

**No. None of the four.** The closest object in the entire set is Schulz & Pfeiffer's `l_{i−}`, which is a *single absolute deadline set once at acceptance*, not a ledger of revisions:

> "We calculate l_{i−} = B_{i−} + l directly after customer i accepted our offer." (Sect. 3.1, p. 549)

It *is* checked as a hard feasibility filter before selection, in both the insertion and the reinsertion path:
> "The tour is feasible if [...] **no delivery location j− is reached later than the latest possible arrival time l_{j−} for the corresponding customer j** and the vehicle capacity Q is never exceeded during the trip." (Sect. 3.2, p. 551)
> "In doing so, the time window of the considered customer has to be respected. This can be reached by setting o = l_{i−} + 1 in Line 2 of the algorithm." (Sect. 4.2, p. 554)

What is genuinely there vs. what your thesis needs:
- **Present:** a hard, per-customer, absolute cap on *total* displacement from the originally promised arrival time. Because `l_{i−}` is anchored to the *first* promised `B_{i−}` and never moves, the constraint is de facto a cumulative cap — an unbounded number of small delays is allowed as long as the running total stays ≤ l. That is the single strongest precedent in these four papers.
- **Absent:** (i) any per-revision accounting or state variable that accumulates displacement (the cap is enforced by a route-schedule feasibility test, not by a ledger); (ii) any *re-communication* of a revised promise, which is what would make revisions countable — "Only B_{i−} and l_{i−} are communicated to the customer"; (iii) monotonicity as an explicit property; (iv) any heterogeneity of the budget (l is "a customer independent time span" — they flag per-customer l only as future work, Sect. 7).

Rauh et al. have a *prefix-freeze* rather than a budget: "the resulting schedule S must remain the same for all stops before τ_i" (Sect. 3.3). Molenbruch is static — no promises exist. Kovacs surveys `l_max ≤ L` (ConVRP), which is a max-deviation bound across *days* for a repeat customer, not an intra-episode cumulative ledger, and it is a *plan-level* bound, not a filter applied to one arriving request. There is also no paper with an explicit "number of revisions" counter.

**Gap you can claim cleanly:** none of these papers tracks or bounds *how many times* a promise is revised, nor accumulates displacement as a decision-state variable; Schulz & Pfeiffer measure realized slippage only ex post (Table 2 "Delay", 181.71/279.87/273.20 s) and report "there can be a substantial variance in the delay of single customers" (Sect. 6.3.5) without bounding the tail beyond the single deadline.

## (b) Which paper comes closest to "repair/reorder the existing route under a stability constraint", and what exactly do they do?

**Schulz & Pfeiffer, Sect. 4.2 (precomputation of reinsertions), is by far the closest.** Exactly:
1. In the idle window between two customer requests, for **every already-accepted customer in every tour**, they test whether that customer can be feasibly moved into **each other tour**, using Algorithm 5 (Appendix C) with the FTS constant-time feasibility test (Appendix B).
2. The stability constraint is enforced during the test by forcing the moved customer's own promised deadline: "the time window of the considered customer has to be respected [...] setting o = l_{i−} + 1", plus all *other* customers' deadlines l_{j−} through the tour feasibility test.
3. Each successful move is cached as a "tour with one customer removed", forming K′. Selection for the new arriving customer then ranges over K ∪ K′ (Sect. 4.1).
4. Anytime: "The precomputation is stopped if the next dynamic customer arrives before it is fully done. The already calculated reinsertions can still be used for the next insert in such a case."
5. Cache validity rule (a stability-aware invalidation): "If the corresponding tours to reinsert have not been changed by the dynamic insert and the current time still allows for the possible route change (i.e. the customer in question has not been and would not have been picked up yet), the reinsertion is still valid." (Sect. 4.2, p. 555)
6. Reordering *within* a tour happens only as a side effect of insertion position choice; there is no intra-route 2-opt / or-opt on accepted customers. Only **one** customer is displaced per repair.
Measured cost of this repair capability: CB0 → CB1 improves R̄ from 39.92% to 38.92% but raises mean promise slippage from 181.71 s to 279.87 s (Table 2) — i.e. **~1 pp of rejection bought with ~98 s of average promise erosion**. That is the cleanest published price-of-repair datapoint in these four papers.

Runners-up:
- **Kovacs et al. [73]** as reported in the survey (Sect. 5.1.2, p. 202) — the only "repair-to-stability" operator described: "In each iteration, a routing plan is generated for each day in the planning horizon regardless of the resulting arrival time consistency. Then, **the maximum arrival time difference is reduced by repeatedly reversing parts of selected routes. The reversal corresponds to a 2-opt move (Lin [79]) on the route that contains the customer with the largest maximum arrival time difference.** [...] This solution approach performs well on ConVRP instances; **the stronger the fluctuations in the customer demand, the larger the advantage of the flexible approach compared to the a priori approach.**" Note the direction: cost-first, then repair *toward* stability — the opposite of a hard pre-filter.
- **Kovacs et al. [74]** (survey Sect. 5.1.2, Fig. 9): "extend this updating strategy by **allowing deviations from the visit sequence given by the template**", i.e. explicitly relaxing a priori sequence-freezing.
- **Molenbruch** has full reorder/relocate/exchange/2-opt/r-4-opt/eliminate under hard service-level constraints, but statically, with no accepted-promise notion.
- **Rauh et al.** only contemplate it: "it is also an option to remove an outbound request from the schedules in case of an unsuccessful insertion of the corresponding inbound trip and try reinsertion for different time windows" — not implemented.

## (c) Any theory: theorems, bounds, guarantees of feasibility / non-emptiness of the candidate set?

**Essentially none for the candidate set. The literature is empirical.** The complete inventory:

**Non-emptiness (the one real guarantee), Schulz & Pfeiffer Sect. 2.4, p. 547:**
> "Third, **our setting always allows to add a new customer at the end of a tour. Thus, there is always a feasible offer for a customer.** Rejection occurs if the customers assess their offer as too unattractive. In the literature, service often needs to happen within time windows (Avraham and Raviv 2021; Hosni et al. 2014; ...)."
This is structural, not proved: it holds because there are no pickup time windows, no waiting, and appending to the tail cannot violate any predecessor's l_{j−}. It is the architectural reason their candidate set is never empty — and precisely the property a cumulative-cap dispatcher must reproduce or replace.

**Complexity / runtime bounds, Schulz & Pfeiffer:**
- Sect. 4.2: "The computational effort to evaluate all inserts for a customer into a tour k of length n̄_k is bounded by O(n̄²_k) [...] O(n · n̄²_k) [...] As Σ_{k=1}^{|K|} n̄_k ≤ 2n, the computational effort to evaluate all possible reinsertions in all tours is O(n³) in the worst case."
- Appendix B: "Given F_{n̄} = l_{σ_{n̄}} − B_{σ_{n̄}}, **all FTSs can be computed recursively in linear time.** [...] In total, feasibility of an insertion can be checked in constant time with linear effort in a preprocessing step." (after Gschwind & Drexl 2019, Savelsbergh 1992.)
- Dominance/pruning lemma stated informally in Appendix C: "given a feasible insertion of pickup and delivery location after positions p and p′, respectively, it is also feasible to insert the pickup location after any position p″ with p < p″ < p′ and t_{σ_{p″},m+} + t_{m+,σ_{p″+1}} − t_{σ_{p″},σ_{p″+1}} < t_{σ_p,m+} + t_{m+,σ_{p+1}} − t_{σ_p,σ_{p+1}}"; plus the triangle-inequality early-exit: "As an insertion of the delivery location at any later point of the tour can never lead to a better solution (triangle inequality), the algorithm jumps to Line 29 and terminates."
- Exactness claim for the lookahead: "**Although we calculate the evaluation of the next insert optimally (given that accepted customers can only be reinserted), the procedure is clearly heuristic**, since it does not concretely consider all future customers beside the next one." (Sect. 2.5) and "given this restriction, the expected probability that the next customer rejects is calculated exactly" (Sect. 2.4).

**Rauh et al.:** only "The resulting problem is both NP-hard and hard to solve in practice, as it is an extension of the classic DARP problem (Parragh et al. 2008)" (Sect. 1). Plus a combinatorial property used by Francis et al. reported via Kovacs.

**Molenbruch:** no theory; statistics only (ANOVA F = 13.313; paired t = −14.839 and −9.945).

**Kovacs survey — the only actual theorems/bounds in the set:**
1. Boolean characterisation of when inconsistency is unavoidable (Sect. 3, p. 195): "Let C, F, R, and I be Boolean variables that are true if cost is minimized (C), demand is significantly fluctuating (F), resource constraints are tight (R), and the routing plans are inconsistent (I). Then, expressed in Boolean algebra, the condition **F ∧ (C ∨ R) is sufficient for I, that is, F ∧ (C ∨ R) ⇒ I.**" And: "Condition F ∧ (C ∨ R) is also necessary for inconsistency, that is, F ∧ (C ∨ R) ⇔ I, **if there is only a single optimal solution to the problem**" — with an explicit counterexample showing inconsistency under constant demand (0-a-b-0 on day 1, 0-b-a-0 on day 2).
2. Feasibility bound on the number of mutually-different plans (Sect. 6, p. 208): "**the number of feasible edge disjoint solutions m is bounded by m ≤ |V| / (2λ) where λ is the minimum number of routes required per day (Ngueveu et al. [86])**."
3. Worst-case cost bound: "Duchenne et al. [42] demonstrate that the optimal cost of a 4-PSP can be up to 250% higher than four times the optimal TSP cost"; "Ngueveu et al. [86] indicate that the difference between m times the optimal VRP cost and the optimal m-PVRP increases with m."
4. Non-equivalence proof: "In Smilowitz et al. [99], **the authors prove that minimizing the average number of different drivers per customer (i.e., optimizing driver consistency) is not equivalent to minimizing the drivers' average learning burden of visiting customers (i.e., optimizing customer consistency).**" (Sect. 5.2, p. 204.) Directly relevant: your "displacement" metric and any "count of revisions" metric will likewise not be co-optimal.
5. Combinatorial route-count guarantee (Sect. 5.2.2, p. 206, Francis et al. [49]): "if S contains |S|−1 schedules without any common day and a schedule that is the union of these schedules, then **the maximum number of different routes each driver has to perform is |S| − 1**."
6. A referenced approximation result (reference list only, no discussion): "[3] A.A. Ageev and A.V. Pyatkin, 'A 2-approximation algorithm for the metric 2-peripatetic salesman problem'."

There is **no** theorem anywhere in these four papers guaranteeing that a stability-constrained candidate set is non-empty, no competitive-ratio analysis of a bounded-promise policy, and no approximation guarantee for consistent VRP/DARP.

## (d) Evidence on how THIN the candidate set is (feasible insertion positions per vehicle per request)?

**No paper reports a count.** This is a real, quotable gap. What does exist is qualitative and indirect:

1. **Kovacs survey, Sect. 5.1.1, p. 201 (Fig. 8) — the sharpest statement, a worked singleton case:**
> "Figure 8 gives an example when customer occurrence is random and the maximum arrival time difference is bounded by zero. There is one vehicle to visit customers. The figure at the top shows a solution when the route for day 1 is planned without information about day 2. **The only feasible insertion position for customer 4 on day 2 is the last visit before the vehicle returns to the depot.** Full information is available in the solution at the bottom. Customer 4 is again inserted into the last position; the routes, however, are more efficient."
Immediately preceded by the general warning: "Using hard constraints, however, may lead to infeasible or unreasonable solutions when demand fluctuations cannot be anticipated."

2. **Schulz & Pfeiffer — the whole architecture is an admission that the direct candidate set is too thin.** Sect. 4.2: "Removing a single already accepted customer and reinsert them into another tour leads to a higher degree of freedom for the insertion of new requests. If a customer is reinserted in a different tour, it is more likely that a new customer can be inserted in the former tour of the reinserted customer." The candidate set is enumerated over |K ∪ K′| tours, |K| ∈ {6,8,10} plus precomputed K′; |K′| is never reported. The tail-append fallback (Sect. 2.4) guarantees |candidates| ≥ 1 always.
   Indirect thinness evidence from the l-sweep (Sect. 6.3.5): at l = 0 the rejection rate is markedly higher than at l = 5 min, and even at l = 30 min "the average fraction of rejected customers does not decrease below 0.38" — i.e. the binding scarcity is not promise-budget but vehicle-time.
   Utilisation dependence (Sect. 6.3.2, Finding 2): "**If there are few vehicles and/or customers arrive very quickly one after another (i.e. the utilization of the system increases), the tours likely do not offer many possibilities for new insertions.**" And the converse: "In the upper right setting, the system utilization is low, i.e. when a new customer arrives there is a high likelihood of being able to insert them into one of the tours in a reasonable way. Here precomputation is not needed."

3. **Rauh et al., Sect. 5.2 — the best qualitative description of candidate-set collapse under a chained commitment:**
> "A detailed examination of the schedules and metrics of GIH_flex reveals that it initially accepts requests and creates efficient schedules with good utilization, but **quickly struggles to insert further requests**. The issue arises because requests are scheduled very tightly together in only a small part of the session as this minimizes the objective of the greedy insertion. However, **this complicates the accommodation of the respective return trips as there is little space for insertion. This leads to the rejection of many requests** and ultimately results in shorter total route durations."
Also, Sect. 5.2: they attribute the low value of extra capacity to a different binding constraint — "even though we increase the vehicle capacity to 5 or higher, it hardly happens that 5 or more patients are on board of a vehicle at the same time [...] One reason could be that the limit on the user ride time is a more limiting constraint during the schedule creation than the vehicle capacity"; and then, on ride-time relaxation, "It seems more likely that the detour potential to combine requests is not larger because of the **resulting inherent difficulty in providing the return trips within time**."

4. **Molenbruch, Sect. 2 (initial solution construction)** — the only operational statement about insertion feasibility, and it is a fallback rather than a count: "They are sequentially inserted at the best possible position across all existing routes, such that total distance traveled is minimized. **Only if no feasible insertion is found, a new route is activated.**" With unlimited fleet the candidate set is trivially non-empty. Consistent with thinness at high service levels: required fleet falls from 11.14 at (0,0.0) to 5.47 at (60,1.0) (Table 4), i.e. tight service levels force ~2× the vehicles because each vehicle admits so few compatible customers.

**Bottom line for the thesis:** no one has published a distribution (or even a mean) of feasible insertion positions per vehicle per request under a stability/consistency constraint. Reporting that distribution, and showing how it shrinks as a cumulative promise budget tightens, is an unoccupied empirical slot — and Schulz & Pfeiffer's `l` sweep (l ∈ {0,5,10,15,20,25,30} min), their Table 2 "Delay" metric, and Kovacs's Fig. 8 singleton example are the three natural hooks for it.
