# RideBound: primary-source audit of business and passenger need

Date: 2026-10-05. Read-only literature assessment; no repository edits or experiments.

## Five primary sources

1. **Fielbaum & Alonso-Mora (2020), Transportation Research C 121, 102831.** Published online 22 October 2020. [Author-hosted final paper](https://autonomousrobots.nl/assets/files/publications/20-Fielbaum-TRC.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trc.2020.102831).

   Two-hour Manhattan simulation, 19,610 taxi requests; no passenger experiment. Defines first-announced versus realized wait/detour/delay and counts prediction increases. Unrestricted runs affect at least approximately one third of initially accepted requests. Critical baseline caveat: Section 4 removes the original Alonso-Mora restriction against increasing reassigned passengers' waiting time. Section 6 already protects first-announced pickup times while permitting same/earlier reassignment and preventing cancellation. Table 5: rejection 34.5% → 45.6%; wait 1.01 → 0.77 minutes; detour 0.65 → 0.94; average weighted user cost 9.56 → 11.6. Quote: “without increasing the rejection rate or passengers’ average delay and waiting times”. This establishes a research problem and an adverse trade-off, not demand for RideBound's particular ledger. Does not propose cumulative absolute decision changes.

2. **Schulz & Pfeiffer (2026), OR Spectrum 48, 541–586.** Published 2 February 2026. [Publisher full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00291-026-00847-0).

   A close competing formulation: immediately after acceptance, sets delivery deadline `l_i-=B_i-+l`, bounding later changes relative to the accepted offer. Quote: “The value of l can be communicated to the customer beforehand”. Customers cannot subsequently be rejected. Reassignments/reoptimization remain permitted inside this deadline. Offer acceptance probability is assumed exponential in relative detour; customer demand benefits are model outcomes, not measured adoption. Experiments use 120 stops from a former Hamburg provider and generated demand, capacities six, 6–10 vehicles, 100–1,000 customers. Authors report too-tight windows cause rejection and too-wide windows discourage acceptance; their model favors 10–15 minutes. A 15-minute bound is described as practice. Explicit provider relevance exists, but no commercial purchase/ROI proof. Adverse implication: communicate and protect a fixed accepted deadline is already prior work; compare ledger with that simpler customer contract.

3. **Alonso-González et al. (2020), Transportation Research C 115, 102621.** Accepted 11 March 2020; June 2020 issue. [Institutional record](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid%3A7e7284dd-0d14-4b1c-96cf-eb1b363f4fe4), [final paper](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_1217abf5-bc01-4227-ba39-21878929e291).

   Stated-preference experiments with 1,006 urban/suburban Dutch respondents, commuting/leisure, waiting/in-vehicle/transfer stages. Quote: “willingness to pay to reduce travel time variability/uncertainty”. In-vehicle value of time €7.88–10.80/hour; value of variability €3.15–5.76/hour; reliability/time ratio about 0.5. Reliability attributes are distributions of experienced times and systematic delay from an announced time. This provides measured preferences for actual journey uncertainty and promise error, not preferences for a sum of same-state marginal route changes. Evidence remains hypothetical choices, not actual payments, retention, or profit. Three of four transfer reliability parameters are not significant at 95%. Their mean-variance approach is explicitly chosen to study offered-service variability rather than individual lateness disutility. Business relevance is explicit service-design use; RideBound demand remains inference.

4. **König & Grippenkoven (2019/2020), European Transport Research Review 12, 1.** Published 31 December 2019. [Publisher full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12544-019-0391-3).

   German stated-choice experiment, 410 respondents; hypothetical shopping/doctor trips. All six attributes (fare, access walking, booking lead time, departure shift, travel time, information) significantly influence choice. Doctor trips increase time sensitivity. Information levels include approximate arrival corridor versus detailed real-time route/arrival. Quote: “travelers should be informed preferably on time about changes of the departure or arrival”. Supports useful visible information and arrival/departure constraints, not freezing a stale estimate. More information can partly compensate for slower travel; preferences are heterogeneous. Highly educated self-selected sample; authors explicitly require field validation and restrict generalization to studied trip purposes. No willingness-to-pay estimate for cumulative decision-induced variation, and no business experiment using RideBound.

5. **Tellez, Vercraene, Lehuédé, Péton & Monteiro (2021/2022), Networks 79, 452–478.** First published 11 June 2021; June 2022 issue. [Publisher](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/net.22063).

   Multiperiod dial-a-ride for people with disabilities; consistency means serving the same passenger at similar times across the planning week. Quote: “the same service time is used all along the planning horizon”. Real application demand data plus computational Pareto-front experiments show costs versus consistency. Explicit vulnerable-passenger service requirement, but not a same-trip announced ETA revision contract, customer-visible variation budget, or realized punctuality guarantee. Important adverse boundary: one cannot import this operational need wholesale to justify RideBound's within-trip ledger, nor claim time consistency as novel. Read publisher abstract only; no numerical gains inferred beyond it.

## Implications for RideBound (inference, not source findings)

- Strongest established need: useful trip information, actual uncertainty, arrival relative to an accepted promise, appointment punctuality, and availability. None of these sources validates willingness to pay for `C=sum |proposedETA-unchangedRouteProjection(currentState)|` as such.
- Plausible internal buyer is a pooled-transport operator's dispatcher/service-quality team only if limiting its own interventions improves a measured customer contract sufficiently to justify rejected requests, detours, vehicle cost, and complexity. This is a hypothesis needing validation; no documented purchaser currently follows from these papers.
- A passenger can suffer lateness while C=0: world-state projection drift is uncharged. An accurate corrective update can be useful. Absolute changes also charge earlier arrivals; value to passengers need not be symmetric. Thus low C cannot stand alone as improved service or on-time performance.
- H6's 7.13 percentage-point coverage loss is a material adverse result, matching known flexibility/consistency trade-offs; bundling locks and drop cap prevents assigning gains solely to the ledger. T38's tie with a simple deadline on served count supplies no demonstrated incremental business advantage. These are parent-provided project facts, not independently re-run here.
- Potential research contribution is a precise operational accounting mechanism plus an honest incremental comparison or negative-result boundary. The research problem is genuine but old; evidence for the particular mechanism's superior business value is currently absent.
